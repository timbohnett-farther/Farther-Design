Liquidity and business transitions
Comparing borrowing, tax cost, concentration risk, and collateral-call risk
Securities-backed lending allows an investor to borrow against a portfolio for liquidity needs without selling and triggering capital gains tax, but it introduces its own risks, including collateral calls if the portfolio's value declines significantly.
How it works
Selling appreciated securities for liquidity triggers capital gains tax immediately, reducing the amount actually available to spend. Borrowing against the same securities instead avoids that tax entirely, but the loan must be serviced, and a significant market decline could trigger a collateral call requiring additional cash or asset sales at an inopportune time.
Step 1
Pledge a portfolio of eligible securities as collateral for a loan.
Step 2
Borrow up to the lender's advance rate against the collateral's value.
Step 3
Use the loan proceeds for the intended liquidity need, without selling the underlying securities.
Step 4
Monitor the loan-to-value ratio, since a market decline could trigger a collateral call.

Securities-Backed Lending vs. Selling Assets · fit and reference
Who this is designed for
Investors with significant appreciated, low-basis securities needing liquidity
Anyone wanting to avoid triggering capital gains tax for a temporary liquidity need
Households comfortable managing the ongoing cost and collateral-call risk of a securities-backed loan
Key benefits at a glance
Avoids triggering capital gains tax on the pledged securities
Preserves the underlying portfolio's continued market exposure and growth potential
Can provide relatively efficient access to liquidity compared to other borrowing options
Useful for bridging temporary liquidity needs without disrupting a long-term investment strategy
Important considerations
Ignoring collateral-call risk
Borrowing near the maximum advance rate without a buffer for market declines.
Treating it as free money
Overlooking the ongoing interest cost of the loan compared to a one-time tax cost from selling.
Concentration compounding
Using this strategy on an already concentrated position, compounding both market and collateral-call risk.
