Business transitions · client education guide
Business transitions
An agreement funded to last year's valuation can fail at this year's event
A buy-sell agreement without funding is often just a promise written during calm years and tested during the worst possible moment. Life insurance is one of the most common ways to fund that obligation.
How it works
The usefulness of the policy depends on whether the valuation, ownership structure, tax treatment, and triggering events all align with the agreement.
Step 1
Refresh the valuation, and confirm the agreement's valuation method still holds.
Step 2
Compare coverage in force against the current buyout obligation.
Step 3
Check that policy owner and beneficiary match the agreement's structure.
Step 4
Re-test after every material change in value, ownership, or the owner group.

Buy-sell funding · fit and reference
What proper funding solves
Immediate liquidity when an owner dies, becomes disabled, or exits unexpectedly
A defensible valuation established before anyone is negotiating under pressure
Continuity for employees, lenders, and customers during the transition
A clean outcome for the departing owner's family, without a claim on the business
The common mismatches
Coverage sized to a valuation that is several years old
Policy owner or beneficiary that does not match the agreement's structure
Triggering events in the document that the funding does not actually cover
A valuation method in the agreement that no longer reflects how the business is priced
Two structures, different mechanics
Entity purchase
The business owns the policies and buys the departing interest. Simpler to administer with several owners.
Cross-purchase
Each owner insures the others and buys directly. Basis treatment differs, and policy count grows quickly.
Either way
The amount, owner, beneficiary, and contractual triggers have to be coordinated, or the funding does not reach the obligation.
