Splitting the number in half does not split the problem in half
Divorce financial planning is not simply about splitting assets in half. Retirement accounts require specific legal orders to divide without triggering taxes or penalties, concentrated stock and business interests need valuation and liquidity planning, and health insurance coverage often needs an immediate solution.
The decisions that actually determine the outcome
Retirement accounts require a QDRO, not just an agreement
Dividing a 401(k) or pension requires a Qualified Domestic Relations Order, a specific court-approved document. Without one, an attempted transfer can trigger immediate taxes and a 10% early withdrawal penalty even if the divorce decree says the account should be split.
Concentrated stock and business interests need real valuation
Equity compensation, a family business, or real estate often cannot simply be split down the middle. They typically require professional valuation, a buyout structure, or an offsetting asset trade.
Health insurance is often the most urgent unresolved item
A non-employed or lower-earning spouse frequently loses employer health coverage at divorce and needs a bridge solution such as COBRA or a marketplace plan arranged before the decree is finalized.

It can require tens of thousands of dollars in additional annual savings per person to stay on track for the same retirement date, a gap that is easy to underestimate during the emotional process of divorce. Why the timeline is the real constraint
A 55-year-old dividing a $3 million retirement portfolio in half has roughly a decade less time to rebuild than someone divorcing at 35. The arithmetic of the split is straightforward. The arithmetic of replacing it on a shorter runway is not, which is why the order of operations, and the tax treatment of each transfer, matters more here than in almost any other life event.
