Estate planning
Titling can quietly override what your will says
Titling is how ownership of an account is legally structured: individually, jointly with rights of survivorship, as tenants in common, or as tenants by the entirety. Each choice changes control, creditor exposure, and what happens at death.
How it works
Titling determines whether an account passes automatically to a co-owner, becomes part of a probate estate, or is exposed to a co-owner's creditors or divorce proceedings. Where it conflicts with a will, titling generally wins.
Step 1
Inventory how every account across the household is currently titled.
Step 2
Compare each title against what your estate plan says should happen.
Step 3
Check state eligibility for tenancy by the entirety or community property treatment.
Step 4
Correct mismatches with your attorney, and revisit after any marriage, divorce, or move.
Seeing it in action

Account titling · fit and reference
What getting titling right buys you
Assets pass to the intended person without unnecessary probate delays
Meaningful creditor and liability protection, depending on structure and state
No unintended disinheritance when a will and account titling conflict
Clearer outcomes for blended families and second marriages
Less family conflict and confusion during an already difficult time
Where state law changes the answer
Tenancy by the entirety exists in roughly 25 states and D.C., and only for married couples
Some of those states limit it to real estate; others extend it to bank and investment accounts
Community property can significantly affect basis step-up at death and divorce outcomes
Tenancy in common exists everywhere, but the probate process for a decedent's share varies by state
The four structures to know
Joint tenancy with survivorship
Equal ownership. A deceased owner's share passes automatically to the survivors, bypassing probate.
Tenancy in common
Shares can be unequal, and each owner's share passes by will or intestacy, not to the co-owner.
Tenancy by the entirety
Married couples in certain states only, adding creditor protection beyond standard joint tenancy.
