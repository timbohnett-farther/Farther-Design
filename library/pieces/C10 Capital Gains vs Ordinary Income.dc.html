Tax planning
One day can change the rate on the same gain
Ordinary income covers wages, interest, and short-term investment gains, taxed at your regular rate. Long-term capital gains, from assets held longer than a year, are taxed at preferential rates that are generally lower.
How it works
Selling one day before rather than after the one-year mark can mean paying your top ordinary rate instead of a substantially lower long-term rate. The code is structured to reward patience.
Step 1
Check the acquisition date on the specific lot you intend to sell.
Step 2
Compare the tax at ordinary rates against the long-term rate for your bracket.
Step 3
Weigh the tax saved against the market risk of holding longer.
Step 4
Apply the same logic to dividends, where qualified and non-qualified are taxed differently.

Holding periods · fit and reference
What the distinction changes
It rewards a longer-term approach, which generally aligns with better outcomes anyway
It lets you plan sale timing around the one-year threshold
It clarifies why qualified and non-qualified dividends are taxed differently
It makes after-tax comparisons between strategies meaningful
It informs decisions around inherited assets, which often receive a step-up in basis
Where the threshold is easy to miss
Multiple lots of the same position acquired on different dates
Shares from vesting or reinvested dividends, each with their own clock
Rebalancing trades executed without checking acquisition dates
Holding a deteriorating position purely to reach the one-year mark
Three rates on the same portfolio
Ordinary income
Wages, interest, and short-term gains, taxed at your marginal rate.
Long-term capital gains
Gains on assets held more than a year, taxed at preferential rates.
Qualified dividends
Taxed at long-term rates where holding-period requirements are met, otherwise at ordinary rates.
