Investing habits · client education guide
Investing habits
A schedule beats a guess about timing
Dollar-cost averaging invests a fixed amount at regular intervals regardless of market direction, rather than trying to place a lump sum at the perfect moment. It buys more shares when prices are low and fewer when they are high.
How it works
The point is less about maximizing return in any single scenario and more about building a sustainable habit that keeps you investing through both up and down markets.
Step 1
Choose an amount you can sustain through a downturn, not just a good year.
Step 2
Set the interval and automate it through payroll or scheduled transfers.
Step 3
Leave the schedule alone when markets move; that is the mechanism working.
Step 4
Revisit the amount when income changes, rather than when markets do.

Dollar-cost averaging · fit and reference
What the habit gives you
No emotional burden of picking the right moment
A naturally lower average cost per share during volatile periods
A consistent savings habit, which retirement plan contributions already use
Less risk of committing a large lump sum right before a downturn
Automatic execution through payroll deductions or scheduled transfers
What it does not do
It does not guarantee a better result than a lump sum in a rising market
It does not protect against loss in a sustained decline
It works only if the schedule survives the months you least want to contribute
Waiting indefinitely for a better entry point is a decision with its own cost
Three approaches to the same money
Dollar-cost averaging
Fixed amounts at fixed intervals. Lowers the average cost in volatile periods and removes the timing decision.
Lump sum
Fully invested immediately. Historically favorable in rising markets, and harder to hold through an early drawdown.
Waiting
Cash on the sidelines earning a cash rate, missing whatever the market does in the meantime.
