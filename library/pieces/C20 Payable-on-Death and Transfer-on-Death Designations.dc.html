Estate planning
A form at the bank can do what probate cannot
A payable-on-death designation names who receives a bank account or CD at your death. A transfer-on-death designation does the same for brokerage accounts and, in many states, for real estate through a TOD deed.
How it works
Like beneficiary designations on retirement accounts, these pass assets directly to a named person outside probate, quickly and at minimal cost. They are simple to set up and frequently overlooked.
Step 1
List every bank, CD, and brokerage account without a named recipient.
Step 2
Add a POD or TOD designation, usually a single form with no attorney required.
Step 3
Confirm whether your state offers a TOD deed, a Lady Bird deed, or neither.
Step 4
Where neither is available, discuss a trust with your attorney instead.

POD and TOD · fit and reference
What these designations give you
Accounts that bypass probate, saving heirs time, cost, and complexity
A change made with a form, usually without an attorney
Clarity that reduces the potential for disputes among heirs
A layer that works alongside a will or trust rather than replacing it
Privacy, since these assets never enter the public probate record
Where state law limits the tool
TOD deeds for real estate are not available in every state
Florida, Michigan, Texas, Vermont, and West Virginia use an enhanced life estate, or Lady Bird, deed instead
New Jersey and Massachusetts do not currently offer a TOD deed option
Where neither exists, a trust is generally the appropriate alternative
Three asset types, three tools
Bank accounts and CDs
A payable-on-death designation, added with a form at the institution.
Brokerage accounts
A transfer-on-death designation, added the same way.
Real estate
A TOD deed where the state allows it, a Lady Bird deed in several states, or a trust.
