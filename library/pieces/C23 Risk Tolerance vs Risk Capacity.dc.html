Portfolio construction
Comfort with risk is not the same as room for it
Risk tolerance is emotional comfort with volatility, how well you sleep when markets drop. Risk capacity is the financial ability to absorb losses without jeopardizing goals, set by horizon, income stability, and reserves.
How it works
The two do not always match. Someone can be comfortable with significant risk and lack the capacity to recover from a large loss, or feel anxious despite having decades to recover.
Step 1
Measure tolerance honestly, through how past declines actually felt.
Step 2
Measure capacity from horizon, income stability, and cash reserves.
Step 3
Where they conflict, build to the more conservative of the two.
Step 4
Reassess both as the financial situation and goals change.

Risk tolerance and capacity · fit and reference
What considering both dimensions prevents
Taking more risk than the financial situation can support
Being too conservative relative to true capacity, which costs growth
A portfolio built on stated preferences rather than actual circumstances
Panic-selling in a downturn, because expectations were realistic to begin with
Conversations about tradeoffs that never get specific
What sets capacity, regardless of comfort
Time horizon, which determines how much recovery time exists
Income stability, which determines whether contributions can continue
Cash reserves, which determine whether investments must be sold in a downturn
The date the money is actually needed
Four combinations, three answers
High tolerance, high capacity
Growth-oriented allocation is appropriate on both counts.
High tolerance, low capacity
Build to capacity. Comfort does not shorten the recovery time available.
Low tolerance, high capacity
Build closer to tolerance, and revisit as confidence and evidence accumulate.
Low tolerance, low capacity
Build conservatively; both dimensions point the same direction, making this the most straightforward case.
