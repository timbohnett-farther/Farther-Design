Tax planning
Pay a known rate now instead of an unknown rate later
A Roth conversion moves money from a traditional IRA or 401(k) into a Roth account. You pay ordinary income tax on the converted amount in that year, and the funds then grow and can be withdrawn tax-free in retirement.
How it works
Conversions work best gradually, filling the current bracket over several years rather than pushing a single year into a much higher one. Low-income years, such as early retirement before Social Security, are the natural window.
Step 1
Identify the years where taxable income is temporarily low.
Step 2
Calculate how much room remains in the current bracket.
Step 3
Convert up to that amount, then stop for the year.
Step 4
Repeat annually, watching Medicare and other income-based thresholds.
Seeing it in action
A retiree who has stopped working but has not yet started Social Security, temporarily in a lower bracket.

Roth conversions · fit and reference
What a strategic conversion does
Locks in today's rate on the converted amount, valuable if rates rise later
Reduces future required distributions, since Roth IRAs have none for the owner
Can lower the tax burden passed to heirs, who often prefer inheriting Roth assets
Creates flexibility to manage taxable income in retirement
Fits naturally into temporary low-income years
What to weigh before converting
Conversions are permanent and cannot be reversed once completed
The converted amount adds to taxable income, which can affect Medicare premiums through IRMAA
A five-year holding period generally applies to each conversion before earnings come out free of tax and penalty
Paying the tax from outside the account preserves more of the converted balance
Three questions that size the conversion
How much bracket room?
The amount that fits before the next rate applies is usually the year's answer.
What thresholds are nearby?
IRMAA and other income-based limits can make the marginal cost higher than the bracket suggests.
Where does the tax come from?
Paying from taxable assets rather than the conversion itself leaves more compounding inside the Roth.
