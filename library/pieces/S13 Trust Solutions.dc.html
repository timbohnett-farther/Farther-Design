Is it time to break with your traditional trust company?
The inheriting generation is asking for different things
The generational transition of wealth in the United States is enormous, unprecedented, and accelerating. A large portion of this asset transition will occur in some form of trust vehicle, and the trusteeship marketplace is innovating rapidly to accommodate the next generation of family and charitable trust beneficiaries.
Prevalent themes among the inheriting generation
Desire for dynamic, technology-driven 24/7/365 communication with the trust administration team
Demand for transparency into the cost of trust administration versus the cost of investment advisory for trust assets
Requirement for specialization in the numerous functions that govern trusts
Insistence upon collaboration among trustees, beneficiaries, and investment advisory providers
A wish to respect the grantor's intent
Need for multiple layers of risk control, to ensure the trust meets the donor's intent and survives for the anticipated trust term
Stipulation that reporting is a modern, state-of-the-art experience
Byproducts of banking consolidation
High rates of turnover of trust and investment officers
Failed accounting and reporting systems integration
Poor user experience, from antiquated and unintegrated technology backbones
Fear of adopting new technologies, because of unknown effects on legacy systems
Inability to retain talent, especially investment management talent
Investment management driven by top-down compliance instead of bottom-up customization

Three trustee models, and two modern structures
Three trustee models
Model
What it means
Captive trust company
Traditional corporate trustees, largely the purview of large national or regional banks, handling both trust administration and trust investment management under one proprietary umbrella
Independent corporate trustee
A rapidly growing network of corporate trustees that specialize in trust administration and governance, while collaborating with beneficiaries and stakeholders to bifurcate investment management to firms like Farther
Individual trustee
An individual, often a family member or a family office executive, appointed to serve alongside a corporate trustee usually with equal power. Can serve stand-alone or in conjunction with a corporate trustee
Delegated versus directed
Structure
How authority is allocated
Delegated trust
The corporate trustee retains authority to manage and administer trust assets, but determines that the best investment results come from outsourcing investment management to a specialist. Trustees outsource the investment decisions but cannot outsource the fiduciary responsibilities, so they must supervise and monitor the investment advisory firm
Directed trust
The trustee is given specific instructions on how to manage and administer the trust assets, performs administrative duties only, and has no responsibility to supervise or monitor the underlying investments. The corporate trustee provides multi-generational administrative continuity, while the grantor directs who manages the assets
Supervisory functions in a delegated trust include formalizing an Investment Policy Statement (IPS) that governs the investment manager, plus daily risk management monitoring. One benefit to the trust and its beneficiaries is that there are now two sets of eyes watching the investments at all times.

Corporate versus individual trustee
Corporate versus individual trustee
Both corporate and individual trustees are responsible for managing and administering trusts in accordance with the terms of the trust agreement and relevant laws and regulations. One key difference is that a corporate trustee is a business typically owned by shareholders, while an individual trustee is not affiliated with a business or other organization, except where the individual trustee is a family office executive. This means an individual trustee is not beholden to any outside interests and is able to make decisions based solely on the best interests of the trust and its beneficiaries.
Another difference is that corporate trustees are often larger and more experienced than individual trustees, and they may have access to a wider range of resources and expertise. This can be particularly useful where the trust is large or complex, or where it involves investments or other financial instruments that require specialized knowledge. Ultimately, the decision will depend on the specific needs and circumstances of the trust and its beneficiaries.
