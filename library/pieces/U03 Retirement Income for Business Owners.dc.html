Retirement income
Your business was the plan. Now it needs one
Most owners spend thirty years reinvesting in the company and comparatively little in retirement accounts. That is usually right while the business compounds faster than a portfolio would. It also means that at the exit, an owner is handed a large sum and a set of decisions they have never had to make.
There is a window most owners do not know exists. Between the exit and the year required distributions begin at 73, income often falls to its lowest point in decades. That gap is the cheapest tax rate you will ever see, and it closes on a schedule nobody negotiates.

Paying the conversion tax from a taxable account does a second job. It moves $1.58 million out of the estate while converting the same dollars, and leaves heirs a Roth balance rather than a ten-year stream of ordinary income.
When this answer reverses
If she moves to a state with no income tax after the exit, waiting can beat converting. If tax rates fall, the same is true. Conversions are irrevocable, they raise income in the year taken, and Medicare premium surcharges are assessed on income from two years earlier, so a large conversion at 61 shows up in premiums at 63. The gap years are an opportunity, not an instruction.

Three points carry the piece
Owners who reinvested everything arrive at an exit with the wrong shape of balance sheet, and the years just before an exit are when the largest deductions are available. The gap between an exit and age 73 is the lowest-rate window most owners will ever have, and it closes on a fixed schedule. And where withdrawals come from matters as much as how much, because the same dollar can be taxed at 23.8% or 40.8% depending on which account it left.
How Farther approaches this
Retirement income for an owner is not a product question, and it is answered badly by anyone who sees only one account. The Intelligent Wealth Platform holds every account, the business, and the taxable portfolio together, which is the only way sequencing can be done correctly, and models conversions against brackets, surcharges, and thresholds before they are executed.
What that looks like in practice
Building qualified plan capacity in the highest-income years before an exit, with your actuary and CPA
Mapping the gap years the moment an exit date becomes visible, then filling brackets deliberately
Sequencing withdrawals so the marginal rate is chosen rather than accepted
Modeling Medicare surcharges on a two-year lag so a conversion does not produce a surprise premium
Coordinating charitable distributions from retirement accounts, which reduce income directly
