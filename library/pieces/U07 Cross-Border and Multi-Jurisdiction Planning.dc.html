International planning
Two countries, two tax systems, one balance sheet
The United States taxes citizens and residents on worldwide income wherever they live, and applies an entirely separate set of rules to people who are not US residents. Those rules are far less generous than most people expect.
Residence and domicile are not the same test
Question

How it is determined

Worldwide income and reporting obligations

Are you US-domiciled for estate tax?
Whether $15 million or $60,000 shelters your estate
Physical presence combined with intent to remain indefinitely
A person can be a US resident for income tax and not domiciled for estate tax, or the reverse. The tests are separate, and the consequences of getting the second one wrong are far larger. US real estate and US-listed shares held directly are generally US-situated. A limited number of countries have estate or gift tax treaties that substitute a proportionate exemption for the $60,000 figure. Most do not.

Three points carry the piece
Residence for income tax and domicile for estate tax are separate tests, and the second carries a two hundred fifty to one difference in outcome. Leaving the US tax system is a taxable event that reaches retirement accounts, unrealized gains, and future gifts to family who stay behind. And the entire advantage sits in the years before a status changes, which is why the conversation has to happen early.
How Farther approaches this
Cross-border planning fails when each country's advisors work only on their own side of the line. Farther coordinates with your counsel and accountants in every relevant jurisdiction rather than assuming someone else has the other half, and holds your full position, including foreign assets, in one view.
