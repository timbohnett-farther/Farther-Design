Family governance
A document that decides before you have to
Per Morningstar's "Mind the Gap" research, over the ten years ended December 2025, US funds and ETFs returned 9.9% annually in aggregate. The average dollar invested in those same funds earned 8.7%. The difference had nothing to do with the funds. It came entirely from when investors bought and sold.
An investment policy statement is the least glamorous document in wealth management and the one specifically designed to close that gap. It is not a forecast. It is a set of decisions made calmly, in writing, that bind the version of you who will be reading headlines at the bottom of a drawdown.

What the numbers look like in practice
A composite family holds a $50 million pool supporting three branches. There is no written policy. Allocation decisions get made in conversations, distributions are negotiated annually, and managers are replaced after disappointing stretches.

What this comparison assumes
The behavior gap is a population average across millions of investors, and applying it to one family is illustrative rather than predictive. What the research establishes is that the gap is real, that it is behavioral rather than structural, and that it is largest in exactly the complicated and volatile holdings sophisticated families tend to own. A policy is not a promise of better returns. It is a commitment device, and its value is equal to the number of times it is followed when someone would rather not.

Three points carry the piece
The distribution rate is the most consequential number in the document, and a taxable family pool needs a higher return than a tax-exempt endowment to sustain the same spending. The gap between what investments return and what investors earn is behavioral, which means it is addressable by process rather than by selection. And the provision that does the most work is the one governing how the policy itself may be changed.
How Farther approaches this
Policy statements are often written once, filed, and never consulted, which makes them documentation rather than governance. The policy is written around your spending requirements rather than a product lineup, and the Intelligent Wealth Platform holds every account and branch in one view, which is the only way a single policy can govern capital spread across trusts, entities, and generations.
What that looks like in practice
Deriving the return objective from actual distributions, inflation, fees, and tax drag rather than a benchmark
Writing rebalancing bands and manager criteria specific enough to be uncomfortable to ignore
Building a liquidity schedule into the policy so capital calls and taxes are anticipated
Reviewing on a set cadence with the next generation present, so it is understood before it is inherited
Requiring a documented amendment process, which is what separates a policy from a preference
