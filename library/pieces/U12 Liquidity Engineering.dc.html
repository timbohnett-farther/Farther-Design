Wealth is not the same thing as access to cash

The risk this table does not show
A portfolio line of credit is secured by the portfolio, and lenders reduce advance rates and issue collateral calls precisely when markets fall. A facility that looked ample at the start of a decline can shrink partway through it. Borrowing also leaves the market exposure fully in place, so a family that borrows against a falling portfolio owns both the decline and the loan. The answer is not to avoid credit. It is to size the facility well below its maximum, keep genuine cash alongside it, and never let a credit line be the only plan.

Three points carry the piece
Liquidity events arrive on schedules you do not set, which means capacity has to exist before the need does. The cheapest liquidity most families own is the low-rate debt already on their balance sheet, and refinancing it away to raise cash is usually the worst available trade. And credit facilities are least reliable exactly when they are most needed, so a line of credit belongs alongside real cash rather than in place of it.
How Farther approaches this
Liquidity planning falls between the advisor who manages the portfolio, the bank that lends against it, and the attorney who drafted the estate documents. Farther earns no compensation from lending arrangements, so a recommendation to borrow or to sell is made on the arithmetic rather than on a credit spread.
What that looks like in practice
Building a twenty-four month obligation calendar covering capital calls, taxes, tuition, and premiums
Sizing the cash reserve to that calendar rather than to a percentage rule
Arranging credit facilities during calm markets and sizing usage well below the maximum
Modeling estate tax liquidity specifically, including insurance and deferral options for business owners
Protecting existing low-rate debt from being refinanced to solve a short-term problem
