A donor advised fund gives the better deduction and the lower cost. A foundation gives control, permanence, and a governance structure the next generation can participate in. Many families use both.
The lever that helps twice
Qualified charitable distributions from an IRA are not deductions. They come out of adjusted gross income entirely, so they never touch the 0.5% floor and they also lower the AGI that the floor, the deduction cap, the net investment income tax, and other thresholds are measured against. For donors over 70 and a half, the limit is $111,000 in 2026.

Three points carry the piece
The One Big Beautiful Bill Act's 2026 rules reduced the tax benefit of routine annual giving, permanently, and the reduction grows with income. Bunching several years of gifts into one clears the annual floor once instead of repeatedly, which is now worth real money on its own. And donating appreciated securities rather than cash remains the single largest unclaimed benefit in charitable planning.
How Farther approaches this
Charitable planning is usually treated as a December task. Farther earns no product-based compensation from giving vehicles and works alongside your CPA and attorney through the year. Tax-intelligent technology identifies the most appreciated positions to donate and the least appreciated to sell, which are opposite answers to what looks like the same question.
What that looks like in practice
Modeling bunched giving against your actual income pattern, including years when income spikes
Selecting specific tax lots to donate so the largest embedded gains are the ones that leave
Routing retirement distributions as qualified charitable distributions where age and circumstances allow
Evaluating whether a fund, a foundation, or a charitable trust fits what the family is building
Coordinating charitable planning with liquidity events, so a business sale and a giving year line up
