**Data point — the longer-horizon 2012 study.** Using a 12-month DCA period, a 60/40 portfolio and rolling **10-year** holding periods, lump-sum investing produced more wealth in **67% of U.S. periods (1926–2011), 67% of U.K. periods (1976–2011) and 66% of Australian periods (1984–2011)**. On $1,000,000 invested, the average U.S. lump-sum ending value was **$2,450,264** versus **$2,395,824** for DCA — **2.3% more**, or $54,440. Stretch the DCA period to 36 months and lump sum won roughly **90%** of rolling U.S. 10-year periods. (Vanguard, "Dollar-cost averaging just means taking risk later," 2012 — https://static.twentyoverten.com/5980d16bbfb1c93238ad9c24/rJpQmY8o7/Dollar-Cost-Averaging-Just-Means-Taking-Risk-Later-Vanguard.pdf)

**Data point — cash is the worst of the three.** In Vanguard's 2023 historical comparison, lump sum beat cash **70%** of the time and cost averaging beat cash **69%** of the time. Easing in is imperfect; sitting in cash is worse. (Vanguard, 2023 — https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/cost_averaging_invest_now_or_temporarily_hold_your_cash.pdf)

## Insight Callouts

INSIGHT 01 — TWO OUT OF THREE, ACROSS SEVEN MARKETS
Investing the whole amount right away beat easing it in about two-thirds of the time in every market Vanguard measured, from the U.S. to emerging markets. The reason is simple: markets rise more often than they fall, so cash held back gives up returns. The longer you stretch the entry, the wider that gap gets.

INSIGHT 02 — WHEN EASING IN IS THE RIGHT ANSWER
Cost averaging won in the worst 5% of markets, and it cut the frequency of a losing first year from 22.4% to 17.6%. If getting all in at once would keep you awake at night — or make you abandon the plan after a bad month — a shorter, scheduled entry buys something real. Just choose it deliberately, and keep it short.
