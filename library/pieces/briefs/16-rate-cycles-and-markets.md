**Notes on method (please keep as a small footnote on the page):**
- Tightening-cycle start/end dates and target-rate levels for cycles 1–12 are as published by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis. Cycle 14 and cycle 15 dates and target ranges are as published by the Federal Reserve Board's official record of open market operations.
- Easing-cycle rate levels use the monthly average federal funds **effective** rate from FRED, because a published official target range did not exist for the earliest cycles. Easing-cycle start/end months are defined as the month of the first rate reduction and the month the rate reached its cycle low.
- S&P 500 total returns are calculated from Robert Shiller's monthly S&P Composite price and dividend series (month-end basis), which is why they are month-aligned rather than day-aligned.
- Cycle 14's 12-month figure and cycle 15's during-cycle figure are **price returns**, not total returns, because dividend data in the Shiller series ends mid-2024. Cycle 15's price return is calculated from the FRED daily S&P 500 series (5,713.64 on Sept. 19, 2024 → 6,901.00 on Dec. 11, 2025).

## Insight Callouts

**INSIGHT 01 — HIKES DIDN'T STOP STOCKS**
Rising rates feel like a reason to sell. The record says otherwise. The S&P 500 delivered a positive total return during all seven Fed hiking campaigns since 1983, averaging about 9% a year while the Fed was actively tightening.

**INSIGHT 02 — WHAT FOLLOWS THE CUT MATTERS MORE**
Rate cuts are not automatically good news. The two easing cycles that began in 2001 and 2007 came with steep losses, because the Fed was cutting into a recession. The 1984, 1989 and 1995 cycles were kinder. The economy, not the policy rate, wrote the difference.
