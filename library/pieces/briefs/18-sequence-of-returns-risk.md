## Topic 18 — Sequence-of-Returns Risk in Retirement

---

## Metadata
- **Category label** (small caps kicker): PLANNING PERSPECTIVE
- **Headline (page title, 3-6 words)**: Same Average, Different Outcome
- **Subhead (one sentence, states exactly what the chart shows)**: Three retirees start with $1 million, withdraw 4% adjusted for inflation, and earn the same 5% average annual return over 30 years — the only difference is the order the returns arrive, and it decides whether one of them runs out of money.

## Headline Stat Boxes (4 boxes, top of page)

1. **STARTING PORTFOLIO** — **$1,000,000** — Each hypothetical retiree begins with the same balance and the same 4% initial withdrawal, adjusted annually for 2.5% inflation. (J.P. Morgan Asset Management, 2026 Guide to Retirement, slide 27, https://am.jpmorgan.com/content/dam/jpm-am-aem/global/en/insights/retirement-insights/guide-to-retirement-us.pdf)

2. **AVERAGE ANNUAL RETURN** — **5%** — Identical for all three retirees across the full 30 years. Only the sequence of the annual returns differs. (J.P. Morgan Asset Management, 2026 Guide to Retirement, slide 27, https://am.jpmorgan.com/content/dam/jpm-am-aem/global/en/insights/retirement-insights/guide-to-retirement-us.pdf)

3. **BEST ENDING BALANCE** — **$1,592,000** — The retiree whose strong returns came first finishes year 30 with more money than they started with. (J.P. Morgan Asset Management, 2026 Guide to Retirement, slide 27, https://am.jpmorgan.com/content/dam/jpm-am-aem/global/en/insights/retirement-insights/guide-to-retirement-us.pdf)

4. **WORST ENDING BALANCE** — **$0** — The retiree whose weak returns came first depletes the portfolio with roughly seven years of the 30-year retirement still to fund. (J.P. Morgan Asset Management, 2026 Guide to Retirement, slide 27, https://am.jpmorgan.com/content/dam/jpm-am-aem/global/en/insights/retirement-insights/guide-to-retirement-us.pdf)

## Primary Chart/Table Data

**[Chart type]:** Line chart — three lines, portfolio value on the vertical axis ($0 to $2,500,000) and "years of spending" 0 through 30 on the horizontal axis. One line per return sequence, with an end-point dollar label on each. Directly beneath it, a small three-row strip of annual-return bars showing each scenario's year-by-year return pattern (this is exactly how J.P. Morgan lays out the original slide, and the two panels together are what makes the point land).

**Panel A — Portfolio values assuming various return sequence scenarios (the featured data):**

| Return sequence scenario | Starting balance | Initial withdrawal | Inflation adjustment | Average annual return | Years of spending | Ending portfolio value |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Great start / bad end | $1,000,000 | 4% ($40,000) | 2.5% per year | 5% | 30 | **$1,592,000** |
| Steadily average | $1,000,000 | 4% ($40,000) | 2.5% per year | 5% | 30 | **$585,000** |
| Bad start / great end | $1,000,000 | 4% ($40,000) | 2.5% per year | 5% | 30 | **$0 — depleted at approximately year 23** |

**Spread between best and worst outcome: $1,592,000. Same average return. Same withdrawals.**

**Panel B — Annual returns by scenario (the shape the designer should reproduce):** J.P. Morgan shows three rows of year-by-year return bars on a −20% to +20% axis. "Great start / bad end" opens with a roughly +20% year and ends with a roughly −20% year. "Steadily average" is a flat row of identical small positive bars. "Bad start / great end" opens with a roughly −20% year and closes with a roughly +20% year. All three average 5%. **Note: J.P. Morgan does not publish the individual annual return values for the three scenarios — only the bar pattern and the 5% average. The year-by-year numbers are therefore "n.a." and the designer should reproduce the visual pattern, not invent values.**

## Secondary chart or data point

**[Chart type]:** Small horizontal bar chart — "what actually happened in history." J.P. Morgan tested a 4% initial withdrawal rate against 69 rolling 30-year periods from 1928 through 2025, using a portfolio of 40% S&P 500 total return and 60% Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate total return, with withdrawals increased annually by CPI. This shows how wide real-world outcomes have been from the very same starting rule.

| Historical ending wealth after 30 years at a 4% initial withdrawal rate | Share of the 69 rolling periods |
|---|---|
| Greater than $0 (money did not run out) | 85% |
| Greater than $1 million | 66% |
| Greater than $2 million | 47% |
| Greater than $3 million | 35% |
| Greater than $4 million | 31% |
| Greater than $5 million | 24% |

Reading of that bottom line: the same 4% rule, applied to the same $1,000,000 starting portfolio, ran out of money in roughly 15% of historical 30-year windows and ended above $5 million in roughly a quarter of them. Sequence is the main reason for that gap.

Source(s): J.P. Morgan Asset Management, *2026 Guide to Retirement*, slide 33, "The 4% rule: Projected outcomes vs. historical experience" — https://am.jpmorgan.com/content/dam/jpm-am-aem/global/en/insights/retirement-insights/guide-to-retirement-us.pdf

**Optional third data point if space allows:** J.P. Morgan's slide 32 tests initial withdrawal rates of 1% through 10% on a $1,000,000 portfolio inflation-adjusted at 2.5% over 35 years. For a 60/40 portfolio, the model puts the likelihood of still having money after 35 years at 95–100% at a 3% initial withdrawal rate, 90–95% at 4%, 65–70% at 5%, and 40–45% at 6%. (J.P. Morgan Asset Management, 2026 Guide to Retirement, slide 32 — https://am.jpmorgan.com/content/dam/jpm-am-aem/global/en/insights/retirement-insights/guide-to-retirement-us.pdf)

## Insight Callouts

**INSIGHT 01 — THE FIRST FIVE YEARS DECIDE A LOT**
While you are saving, the order of returns barely matters. Once you are spending, it matters enormously. A weak market early forces you to sell more shares to fund the same lifestyle, and those shares never come back to recover.

**INSIGHT 02 — AVERAGES HIDE THE REAL RISK**
All three retirees earned 5% a year on average. One ended with $1.6 million. One ended with nothing, seven years short. This is why a retirement plan needs a spending strategy and a cash reserve, not just a return assumption.

## Quote
"Two investors may enjoy the same average return on the investments in their portfolio, but may still experience very different outcomes on account of the sequence in which these returns arrive." — Wade D. Pfau, Ph.D., CFA, Professor of Retirement Income, The American College, "The Lifetime Sequence of Returns: A Retirement Planning Conundrum" (https://www.njstatelib.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Sequence-of-Returns-Risk-White-Paper.pdf)
