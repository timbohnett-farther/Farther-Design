# GRATs: Moving Future Appreciation With Minimal Gift-Tax Use

**CLIENT EDUCATION GUIDE**

## Grantor Retained Annuity Trusts for volatile or fast-growing assets

A Grantor Retained Annuity Trust (GRAT) lets a grantor transfer an asset into trust, receive back a stream of annuity payments equal to the asset's original value plus a government-set interest rate, while any appreciation above that rate passes to heirs with minimal gift tax cost.

## The Core Idea

If a GRAT's contributed asset appreciates faster than the IRS hurdle rate, the excess appreciation passes to the remainder beneficiaries at the end of the term, with little or no additional gift tax, because the annuity payments returned to the grantor are structured to equal the original contribution plus the hurdle rate. This makes GRATs especially attractive for volatile, fast-growing, or undervalued assets.

## How It Works

**STEP 1.** Contribute an asset expected to appreciate faster than the current IRS hurdle rate into the GRAT.

**STEP 2.** The trust pays the grantor an annuity stream over the trust's term, calculated to return the original value plus the hurdle rate.

**STEP 3.** If the asset outperforms the hurdle rate, the excess appreciation remains in the trust at the end of the term.

**STEP 4.** That excess passes to the remainder beneficiaries, typically with minimal gift tax cost.

## Asset Growth Above the Hurdle Rate

A $5,000,000 asset is contributed to a 2-year GRAT with a 5% hurdle rate, and grows at an actual rate of 20% annually.

| Outcome | Amount |
|---|---|
| Annuity returned to grantor (value + hurdle) | ~$5,512,500 |
| Trust value at end of term (at 20% actual growth) | ~$7,200,000 |
| Excess appreciation passing to heirs | ~$1,687,500 |


*Hypothetical illustration only. Assumes consistent 20% annual growth; actual asset performance and hurdle rates vary and are not guaranteed.*

## Who This Is Designed For

- Owners of volatile, fast-growing, or temporarily undervalued assets
- Founders and business owners anticipating significant near-term appreciation
- Households wanting to transfer future appreciation to heirs with minimal gift tax exemption use

## Key Benefits at a Glance

- Transfers asset appreciation above the hurdle rate to heirs with little or no gift tax cost
- Can be structured as a 'zeroed-out' GRAT, using minimal gift tax exemption at funding
- Well suited to volatile or rapidly appreciating assets
- Can be repeated with successive short-term GRATs to capture ongoing appreciation

## Important Considerations

**Mortality risk.** If the grantor dies during the GRAT term, the strategy's benefit is generally lost, favoring shorter terms.

**Underperformance risk.** If the asset fails to outperform the hurdle rate, no appreciation passes to heirs, though little is lost beyond planning costs.

**Overly long terms.** Choosing an unnecessarily long term, increasing mortality risk without a corresponding benefit.
