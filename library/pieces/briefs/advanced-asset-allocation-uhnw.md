## Allocation Built on the Whole Position

### The measurement problem

Traditional allocation answers a narrow question: how should the money in this account be divided? At $10 million and above, that account is usually a minority of the household. The operating business, vested equity compensation, directly held real estate, and carried interest all carry risk, and most of that risk is equity risk. A portfolio that reads 60/40 on a statement can sit inside a household running above 80% equity exposure.

Advanced allocation starts by measuring exposure across the full position, then using the liquid sleeve deliberately, as a counterweight rather than a standalone portfolio.

### Where the concentration came from

The Russell 1000 Growth Index is more concentrated still, with its top ten holdings representing over 60% of index weight. Passive exposure is no longer neutral exposure.

Endowments reported a 10.9% return in FY25 and a 7.7% ten-year average. The larger institutions express equity risk through private equity and venture rather than long-only public exposure.

Direct indexing sits at 18% adoption despite offering tax-loss harvesting that pooled funds structurally cannot. At this wealth level, that gap is one of the clearest unclaimed advantages on the board.

### What a full-position view gives you

- A real equity exposure number, measured across the business, the equity comp, and the portfolio together
- An answer on overlap. If your employer is a top-ten index name, your index funds and your RSUs are the same bet.
- A liquid sleeve doing the job it is best suited for, absorbing shocks the illiquid side cannot
- A funding path for concentration reduction that uses harvested losses rather than raw gain recognition
- Diversifiers selected for behavior rather than category, since the median rolling five-year stock-bond correlation since 1978 has been 0.36, not negative
- A rebalancing discipline that accounts for assets you cannot trade

### Why this matters now

Index concentration is at a multi-decade high, the correlation that made bonds reliable ballast has been inconsistent since 2022, and private market structures are reaching individual investors faster than most allocations have been rewritten to use them. None of that argues for abandoning public equities. It argues for knowing your total exposure before the next drawdown tells you.

## What the Numbers Look Like in Practice

A composite household: a founder in her late forties, spouse an executive at a public technology company, combined net worth $40 million. The advised portfolio is $12 million invested 60/40. On paper, balanced.

### True exposure versus perceived exposure

| Holding | Value | Equity-like risk |
|---|---|---|
| Advised portfolio, public equity sleeve | $7.2M | Yes |
| Advised portfolio, bonds and cash | $4.8M | No |
| Operating business | $20.0M | Yes |
| Vested employer stock | $6.0M | Yes |
| Home equity | $2.0M | No |
| **Total net worth** | **$40.0M** | |
| **Equity-like exposure** | **$33.2M** | **83%** |

The statement says 60% equity. The household is at 83%.

### The cost of no action

| In a 30% equity decline | Perceived | Actual |
|---|---|---|
| Assets exposed | $7.2M | $33.2M |
| Loss | $2.2M | $10.0M |
| Loss as share of net worth | 5% | 25% |

The second-order problem is timing. The same conditions that mark down a private business tend to mark down public equities and compress distributions and bonuses at once. The drawdown and the income interruption arrive together.

### The benefit of acting now

Three moves, none of which require selling the company.

| Action | Effect |
|---|---|
| Reduce vested employer stock from $6.0M to $2.0M over three years | Removes $4.0M of single-name risk that duplicates index exposure |
| Reposition the liquid sleeve to 35% public equity, weighted away from mega-cap overlap | Turns the portfolio into a counterweight rather than an amplifier |
| Implement direct indexing on the public sleeve | Generates harvested losses that offset the gains created by the reduction |

### Side by side

| Measure | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Equity-like exposure | $33.2M | $27.6M |
| As share of net worth | 83% | 69% |
| Loss in a 30% equity decline | $10.0M | $8.3M |
| Loss as share of net worth | 25% | 21% |
| Single-name employer stock | $6.0M | $2.0M |
| Liquid assets outside equity risk | $4.8M | $10.4M |

The headline is not the reduction in modeled loss. It is the last line. The family more than doubled the capital available to absorb a shock, fund a capital call, or bridge a year of compressed business income, without touching the asset that generates their wealth.

*Assumptions: hypothetical 30% simultaneous decline across public and private equity-like assets, private business valued on public comparables, employer stock reduced ratably over three years, and taxes on the reduction not modeled. Actual tax cost would reduce net proceeds. Diversification and asset allocation do not ensure a profit or protect against loss in declining markets. No allocation prevents loss.*
