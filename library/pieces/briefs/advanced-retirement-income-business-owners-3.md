# Intelligent Wealth Series: Advanced Retirement Income and Distribution Strategies for Business Owners

## Your Business Was the Plan. Now It Needs One.

**For owners approaching or past an exit, converting a lifetime of retained earnings into an income stream that outlasts them.**

Most business owners spend thirty years reinvesting in the company and comparatively little in retirement accounts. That is usually the right call while the business is compounding faster than a portfolio would. It also means that at the moment of exit, an owner is handed a large sum of money and a set of decisions they have never had to make.

There is a window most owners do not know exists. Between the exit and the year required distributions begin at 73, income often falls to its lowest point in decades. That gap is the cheapest tax rate you will ever see, and it closes on a schedule nobody negotiates.

This is the final piece in this series. It is also where most of the earlier ones arrive.

## Before the Exit, and After

### What you can put away in 2026

| Vehicle | 2026 limit |
|---|---|
| 401(k) elective deferral | $24,500 |
| Catch-up contribution, age 50 and over | $8,000 |
| Enhanced catch-up, ages 60 through 63 | $11,250 |
| Combined employer and employee, defined contribution | $72,000 |
| Individual retirement account | $7,500 |
| Defined benefit maximum annual benefit | Lesser of $290,000 or highest three-year average compensation |
| Cash balance plan lifetime accumulation | Approximately $3.7 million |

### A change that took effect this year

| Rule effective January 1, 2026 | Who it affects |
|---|---|
| Catch-up contributions must be made on a Roth basis | Anyone who earned more than $150,000 in wages from the sponsoring employer in the prior year |

Pre-tax contributions remain available up to the standard deferral limit. Above that, the catch-up must be Roth. For a high-earning owner this is a modest tax increase today and a meaningful benefit later, and it happens whether or not anyone mentions it.

### Why owners should look hard at a cash balance plan

| Feature | Defined contribution alone | Adding a cash balance plan |
|---|---|---|
| Annual capacity | Up to $72,000 plus catch-up | Substantially higher, scaling with age |
| Lifetime accumulation ceiling | Not specified | Roughly $3.7 million |
| Best suited to | Any owner | Older owners with consistent profitability |
| Principal constraint | None | Requires funding in most years and covering eligible employees |

For an owner in their late fifties with strong and stable earnings, this is often the largest deduction available anywhere in their plan. It also builds the retirement asset that a business-heavy balance sheet is usually missing.

### The three phases, and the one nobody plans for

| Phase | Typical ages | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| Accumulation | 45 to 60 | Business profit funds qualified plans |
| **The gap** | **Exit to 73** | **Income falls, before distributions and Social Security begin** |
| Distribution | 73 onward | Required distributions start and brackets fill quickly |

The middle row is the opportunity. An owner who exits at 62 and reaches required distributions at 73 has eleven years during which taxable income may be a fraction of what it was, and a fraction of what it will be again.

### Where the money should come from, and when

| Source | Tax character | Generally drawn |
|---|---|---|
| Taxable brokerage | Long-term gains at 23.8%, with a basis adjustment at death | Early, alongside loss harvesting |
| Tax-deferred accounts | Ordinary income at up to 40.8% | Filled to a target bracket, converted during the gap |
| Roth accounts | Not taxed | Last, and preserved for heirs |
| Sale proceeds | Capital gain, possibly excluded under the small business stock rules | At the exit |

### What a deliberate distribution plan gives you

- Retirement assets built before the exit, using the largest deductions available to an owner
- Conversion capacity used during the years when your rate is lowest
- Required distributions reduced before they begin, rather than managed after
- Medicare premium surcharges anticipated, since they are set on income from two years prior
- A Roth balance for heirs, who otherwise inherit a ten-year distribution window at their own top rates
- Charitable distributions routed from retirement accounts, where they reduce income directly

### Why this matters now

Two calendars are running. The gap years are finite and they do not extend. And the Roth catch-up requirement arrived in 2026, which means the mechanics of contributing changed this year for most owners reading this.

There is a larger point worth making at the end of a series like this one. Nearly everything covered across these twenty topics converges here. The balance sheet you mapped, the concentration you reduced, the entity you discounted, the trust you funded, the liquidity you arranged, and the policy you wrote all exist so that this phase works. This is the part the rest was for.

## What the Numbers Look Like in Practice

A composite owner sells her business at 62 and rolls $8 million into an individual retirement account. Required distributions begin at 73. She has eleven gap years and does not use them.

### The cost of no action

| Measure | Amount |
|---|---|
| Rollover balance at 62 | $8,000,000 |
| Balance at 73, growing at 6% | $15,186,000 |
| First required distribution at 73 | About $573,000 |
| Character of every dollar withdrawn | Ordinary income |
| **Embedded tax at a 37% rate** | **$5,619,000** |

The account did well. That is precisely the problem. Growth inside a tax-deferred account is growth in a partnership with the government, and the government's share grew too.

### The benefit of acting now

Converting $600,000 each year through the eleven gap years, paying the tax from taxable assets.

| Measure | Amount |
|---|---|
| Converted over eleven years | $6,600,000 |
| Effective rate on those conversions | 24% |
| Tax paid during the gap years | $1,584,000 |
| Traditional balance remaining at 73 | $6,203,000 |
| Roth balance at 73 | $8,983,000 |
| First required distribution at 73 | About $234,000 |

### Side by side

| Measure | No conversion | Conversion through the gap |
|---|---|---|
| Rollover at 62 | $8,000,000 | $8,000,000 |
| Amount converted | $0 | $6,600,000 |
| Tax paid during the gap years | $0 | $1,584,000 |
| Traditional balance at 73 | $15,186,000 | $6,203,000 |
| Roth balance at 73 | $0 | $8,983,000 |
| Remaining embedded tax at 37% | $5,619,000 | $2,295,000 |
| First required distribution | $573,000 | $234,000 |
| **Total tax on these assets** | **$5,619,000** | **$3,879,000** |
| **Difference** | | **$1,740,000** |

Paying the conversion tax from a taxable account does a second job. It moves $1.58 million out of the estate while converting the same dollars, and it leaves heirs a Roth balance rather than a ten-year stream of ordinary income.

### When this answer reverses

If she moves to a state with no income tax after the exit, waiting can beat converting. If tax rates fall, the same is true. Conversions are irrevocable, they raise income in the year taken, and Medicare premium surcharges are assessed on income from two years earlier, so a large conversion at 61 shows up in premiums at 63.

The gap years are an opportunity, not an instruction. What makes them valuable is that they are finite and that the arithmetic is knowable in advance.

*Assumptions: 6% annual growth, a 24% effective federal rate on conversions and 37% on later distributions, conversion tax paid from taxable assets rather than from the account, eleven gap years, and no state income tax. Required distribution figures use current life expectancy factors. Rates, distribution ages, and life expectancy tables are subject to change. Conversions are irrevocable and increase adjusted gross income in the year taken, which can affect Medicare premiums, credits, and other thresholds. State tax treatment varies materially and can reverse this result.*

Three points carry the piece. Owners who reinvested everything into the business arrive at an exit with the wrong shape of balance sheet, and the years just before an exit are when the largest deductions are available. The gap between an exit and age 73 is the lowest-rate window most owners will ever have, and it closes on a fixed schedule. And where withdrawals come from matters as much as how much comes out, because the same dollar can be taxed at 23.8% or 40.8% depending on which account it left.
