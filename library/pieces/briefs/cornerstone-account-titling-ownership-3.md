## Account Titling and Ownership

### 📜 What It Is
Account titling refers to how ownership of an account or asset is legally structured — for example, individually, jointly with rights of survivorship, as tenants in common, or as tenants by the entirety. Each structure has different implications for control, creditor protection, and what happens at death.

### 📜 Why It Matters
Titling determines whether an account passes automatically to a co-owner, becomes part of a probate estate, or is exposed to a co-owner's creditors or divorce proceedings — often overriding what your will says.

### 📜 Key Structures to Know
- **Joint Tenancy with Right of Survivorship (JTWROS):** Co-owners share equal ownership; when one dies, their share passes automatically to the surviving owner(s), bypassing probate.
- **Tenancy in Common (TIC):** Co-owners can hold unequal shares, and each owner's share passes according to their will or state intestacy law — it does NOT automatically go to the co-owner.
- **Tenancy by the Entirety (TBE):** Available only to married couples in certain states, offering additional creditor protection beyond standard joint tenancy.
- **Community Property:** In certain states, most assets acquired during marriage are considered equally owned by both spouses regardless of whose name is on the account.

## Benefits of Getting Titling Right
- Ensures assets pass to the intended person without unnecessary probate delays
- Can provide meaningful creditor and liability protection depending on the structure and state
- Prevents unintended disinheritance when a will and account titling conflict
- Supports clearer, more predictable outcomes for blended families or second marriages
- Reduces family conflict and confusion during an already difficult time

## State-Specific Considerations
Titling rules and options vary meaningfully by state, and this is an area where "one size fits all" advice can create real problems:

- **Tenancy by the Entirety** is available only in certain states (including Florida, Michigan, New York, Pennsylvania, Virginia, and roughly 20 others plus D.C.) and generally only for married couples; some of these states limit it to real estate only, while others extend it to bank and investment accounts as well.[1]
- **Community Property States** (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin) treat most assets acquired during marriage as jointly owned regardless of whose name appears on the account — this can significantly affect basis step-up at death and divorce outcomes.[2] Alaska, South Dakota, and Tennessee allow couples to voluntarily "opt in" to community property treatment.
- **Tenancy in Common** is available in all states, but the specific probate process for a deceased owner's share varies significantly by state law.

Consider a widow, Sarah, and her adult son who co-own a $500,000 investment account.

| Titling Structure | What Happens When Sarah Passes Away |
|---|---|
| Joint Tenancy with Right of Survivorship | Account passes automatically and immediately to her son, bypassing probate |
| Tenancy in Common | Sarah's 50% share goes through probate and passes according to her will — possibly to someone other than her son if the will says otherwise |

The difference in titling alone could mean the account transfers seamlessly in days, or gets tied up in probate for months.
