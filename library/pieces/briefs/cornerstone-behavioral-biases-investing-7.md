## Behavioral Biases in Investing

### 🧠 What They Are
Behavioral biases are predictable, systematic patterns in how humans make financial decisions — often driven by emotion rather than logic — that can lead to costly investing mistakes even among smart, well-informed people.

### 🧠 Why It Matters
Studies consistently show that the average investor underperforms the very funds they invest in, largely due to poorly timed buying and selling driven by emotion rather than strategy.

### 🧠 The Key Insight
Recognizing these biases in yourself is the first step to building safeguards — like a written investment plan — that help you act rationally even when emotions run high.

## Common Biases to Recognize
- **Loss aversion:** Feeling the pain of losses more intensely than the pleasure of equivalent gains, leading to panic-selling during downturns
- **Recency bias:** Overweighting recent events (a market crash or rally) when predicting the future
- **Herd behavior:** Following what everyone else is doing, rather than sticking to your own strategy
- **Overconfidence:** Believing you (or a trend) can reliably predict market direction
- **Confirmation bias:** Seeking out information that supports a decision you've already made, while ignoring contrary evidence

## Benefits of Recognizing Your Own Biases
- Reduces the likelihood of panic-selling during market downturns
- Helps you avoid buying into hype-driven trends near their peak
- Supports more disciplined, rules-based decision-making
- Encourages the use of a written plan as a safeguard against emotional decisions
- Improves long-term outcomes simply by reducing unnecessary trading activity

## Seeing It in Action

> 📊 **CHART PLACEHOLDER:** Line chart showing market decline and recovery, with markers for 'Investor A panic-sells' and 'Investor B stays invested'

> 💡 **KEY STAT CALLOUT:** Panic-selling during downturns is one of the most common wealth-destroying mistakes


Consider two investors during a sharp market downturn.

| Investor | Behavior | Illustrative Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Investor A | Panic-sells during the downturn, driven by loss aversion | Locks in losses and often re-enters the market later, after much of the recovery has occurred |
| Investor B | Sticks to a written investment plan despite the downturn | Participates fully in the eventual market recovery |

Investor B's advantage came entirely from behavior, not investment selection — both held similar portfolios before the downturn began.
