## Paying Down Debt vs. Investing

### ⚖️ What It Is
This is the common question of whether extra cash should go toward paying down debt faster or be invested for long-term growth — and the right answer often comes down to comparing your debt's interest rate to your expected investment return.

### ⚖️ Why It Matters
High-interest debt (like credit cards, often charging well above 15–20% annually) is a "guaranteed" cost that's very difficult for typical investment returns to beat. Lower-interest debt (like many mortgages) may make more sense to pay down gradually while investing extra funds instead.

### ⚖️ The Key Insight
There's no universal rule — it's a math and psychology exercise weighing your specific interest rates, tax treatment of the debt, expected investment returns, and your comfort with carrying debt.

## Benefits of a Deliberate Approach
- Prioritizes eliminating high-interest debt, which reliably outperforms typical investment returns
- Preserves the ability to invest and benefit from long-term compounding on lower-interest debt
- Reduces financial stress by having a clear, rules-based framework rather than guessing
- Considers the psychological value of being debt-free, not just the pure math
- Accounts for tax-deductibility differences between certain types of debt

## Seeing It in Action

> 📊 **CHART PLACEHOLDER:** Bar chart comparing interest rate on credit card debt (22%) vs. typical investment return (7%)

> 💡 **KEY STAT CALLOUT:** 22% guaranteed debt cost vs. ~7% expected investment return


Consider $10,000 in extra cash available to either pay down debt or invest.

| Debt Type | Interest Rate | Comparison to Typical Investment Return (~7%) |
|---|---|---|
| Credit card debt | 22% | Paying this down is nearly always the better move — few investments reliably beat 22% |
| Mortgage debt | 6% | Closer call — investing may be reasonable if expected returns exceed the after-tax mortgage rate |

The higher the interest rate on the debt, the more strongly the math favors paying it down before investing extra funds.
