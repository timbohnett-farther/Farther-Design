## IRA Contribution Rules and Limits

### 📅 What It Is
An Individual Retirement Account (IRA) is a tax-advantaged account you can open independently of an employer. Contributions to Traditional and Roth IRAs are subject to annual limits, and Roth IRA eligibility phases out at higher income levels.

### 📅 Why It Matters
Unlike 401(k)s, IRA contribution limits are the same regardless of income for Traditional IRAs, but Roth IRA contributions are restricted or eliminated entirely above certain income thresholds — making planning around eligibility important.

### 📅 The Key Insight
You generally have until the tax filing deadline (typically mid-April of the following year) to make an IRA contribution for the prior tax year, giving you a valuable planning window even after the calendar year ends.

## Benefits of Understanding IRA Rules
- Provides an additional tax-advantaged savings vehicle beyond employer plans
- Allows contributions up until the tax filing deadline, creating planning flexibility
- Roth IRA earnings grow completely tax-free if qualified distribution rules are met
- Traditional IRA contributions may be tax-deductible depending on income and workplace plan access
- Understanding income phase-outs helps identify whether a "backdoor Roth" strategy may apply

## Current Limits (2026)
For 2026, the IRA contribution limit is $7,500 for those under 50, with a catch-up contribution bringing the total to $8,600 for those 50 and older.[1] This limit applies to the combined total of Traditional and Roth IRA contributions, not each separately. Income eligibility for direct Roth IRA contributions phases out at higher income levels and should be confirmed annually, as thresholds are adjusted for inflation.
