## Payable-on-Death and Transfer-on-Death Designations

### 🔑 What They Are
A Payable-on-Death (POD) designation applies to bank accounts and CDs, naming who receives the funds when you pass away. A Transfer-on-Death (TOD) designation works similarly for brokerage accounts and, in many states, for real estate through a TOD deed.

### 🔑 Why It Matters
Like beneficiary designations on retirement accounts, POD and TOD designations allow assets to pass directly to a named person outside of probate — often quickly and at minimal cost.

### 🔑 The Key Insight
These designations are simple to set up (usually just a form at your bank or brokerage) but are frequently overlooked, leaving accounts to go through probate unnecessarily.

## Benefits of Using POD/TOD Designations
- Allows accounts to bypass probate, saving time, cost, and complexity for your heirs
- Simple to establish or change — often just a form, no attorney required
- Provides clarity and reduces potential disputes among heirs
- Can be used alongside a will or trust as an additional layer of planning
- Keeps certain assets private, since they don't become part of the public probate record

## State-Specific Considerations
TOD deeds for real estate are not available in every state. As of recent surveys, roughly 30 states plus the District of Columbia allow some form of TOD deed for real property, including states like Arizona, California, Colorado, Illinois, Texas, and Virginia.[1] A handful of states, including Florida, Michigan, Vermont, and West Virginia, instead offer a similar tool often called a "Lady Bird deed" or enhanced life estate deed.[1] Notably, some larger states — including New Jersey and Massachusetts — do not currently offer a TOD deed option for real estate, so alternative strategies like a trust may be more appropriate there.
