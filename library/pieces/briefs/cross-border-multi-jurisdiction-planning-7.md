## Two Countries, Two Tax Systems, One Balance Sheet

**For families with citizenship, residence, property, or heirs in more than one country, where the rules do not simply add up.**

The United States taxes its citizens and residents on worldwide income no matter where they live. It also applies an entirely separate set of rules to people who are not US residents, and those rules are far less generous than most people expect.

A US citizen can shelter $15 million from federal estate tax. A non-resident who is not domiciled in the United States can shelter $60,000 of US-situated assets. That is not a typo, and it is not indexed. Families whose lives cross a border rarely discover this before it matters. The good news is that almost everything in this area responds to planning, and almost none of it responds to hindsight.

## Two Questions Decide Everything

### Residence and domicile are not the same test

| Question | What it governs | How it is determined |
|---|---|---|
| Are you a US person for income tax? | Worldwide income and reporting obligations | Citizenship, green card, or the substantial presence day-count test |
| Are you US-domiciled for estate tax? | Whether $15 million or $60,000 shelters your estate | Physical presence combined with intent to remain indefinitely |

A person can be a US resident for income tax and not domiciled for estate tax, or the reverse. The tests are separate, they are applied separately, and the consequences of getting the second one wrong are far larger.

### The gap most families never see

| Status | Federal estate tax exemption on US-situated assets |
|---|---|
| US citizen or domiciliary | $15,000,000 |
| Non-resident, non-domiciled | $60,000 |

US real estate and US-listed shares held directly are generally US-situated for this purpose. A limited number of countries have estate or gift tax treaties with the United States that can substitute a proportionate exemption for the $60,000 figure. Most do not.

### The other spouse rule

Transfers between spouses are unlimited when both are US citizens. When the receiving spouse is not a US citizen, the unlimited marital deduction does not apply. Lifetime gifts to a non-citizen spouse are capped at $194,000 in 2026, and passing assets at death without a qualified domestic trust can trigger estate tax that a citizen couple would never face.

### Leaving is its own tax event

| Covered expatriate test | 2026 threshold |
|---|---|
| Worldwide net worth | $2,000,000 or more, not indexed for inflation |
| Average annual US income tax over the prior five years | More than $211,000 |
| Certification of five years of full tax compliance | Any failure triggers covered status automatically |

Meeting any one test is enough. The third catches people who are nowhere near the financial thresholds, because it includes every international information return, not just income tax filings.

| Asset type | Treatment on expatriation |
|---|---|
| Marketable securities and most property | Deemed sold the day before, gain above $910,000 taxed |
| IRAs and 401(k) balances | Treated as an immediate lump sum, ordinary rates, no exclusion |
| Deferred compensation | Separate rules, often subject to withholding |
| Non-grantor trust interests | Separate rules again |
| Net investment income tax | Does not apply to the deemed sale gain |

This applies to long-term green card holders as well, meaning anyone who held the card in at least eight of the last fifteen tax years. Letting a green card quietly lapse can be an expatriating act.

### The tax that follows the gift

Gifts and bequests from a covered expatriate to a US person are taxed at 40%, paid by the recipient rather than the giver. Final regulations and the reporting form arrived in 2025. A parent who expatriates and later leaves assets to a child living in the United States creates a liability for that child.

### The reporting layer

| Form | What it covers |
|---|---|
| FinCEN 114 | Foreign financial accounts exceeding $10,000 in aggregate |
| Form 8938 | Specified foreign financial assets above threshold |
| Forms 3520 and 3520-A | Foreign trusts and large foreign gifts |
| Form 5471 | Interests in controlled foreign corporations |
| Form 8854 | Expatriation and covered expatriate certification |

Penalties in this area are assessed per form, per year, and are frequently larger than the tax that would have been owed.

### What coordinated cross-border planning gives you

- Clarity on your actual status under both tests, which is the foundation for everything else
- US-situated assets identified and structured before they become an estate tax problem
- A qualified domestic trust in place where a non-citizen spouse is involved
- Expatriation modeled years ahead rather than months, when the levers still work
- Foreign investments screened for punitive US treatment before they are purchased
- A reporting calendar that keeps the compliance certification clean

### Why this matters now

Two forces are pushing this from a niche concern into a common one. Families are more internationally distributed than they were a generation ago, with children studying, working, and settling abroad. And enforcement infrastructure has matured, with information sharing between tax authorities now routine rather than exceptional.

The planning window is the part people miss. Nearly every effective technique here requires action before a status changes, before a property is purchased, or before a departure date. After the fact there is compliance, and compliance is not planning.

## What the Numbers Look Like in Practice

A composite individual has held a green card for fourteen years, has a worldwide net worth of $12 million including $4 million of unrealized gains, holds $1.5 million in US retirement accounts, and plans to return to their home country.

### The cost of no action

Abandoning the green card without a plan.

| Measure | Amount |
|---|---|
| Worldwide net worth | $12,000,000 |
| Covered expatriate status | Yes, the $2 million test is met |
| Unrealized gain on worldwide assets | $4,000,000 |
| Less the 2026 exclusion | $910,000 |
| Taxable deemed sale gain | $3,090,000 |
| Tax at 20% | $618,000 |
| Retirement accounts deemed distributed | $1,500,000 |
| Tax at ordinary rates | $555,000 |
| **Total federal tax on departure** | **$1,173,000** |

Plus a lasting consequence. Future gifts or bequests to any child who remains in the United States would carry a 40% tax paid by that child.

### The benefit of acting now

Twenty-four months of sequencing before the departure date.

| Action | Effect |
|---|---|
| Harvest losses across the portfolio before the deemed sale date | Reduces the gain that crystallizes at expatriation |
| Distribute or convert retirement balances across earlier, lower-income years | Avoids a single year at the top ordinary rate |
| Verify five years of returns and every international information form | Prevents automatic covered status on a technicality |
| Complete gifts to US-resident children before expatriation | Avoids the 40% recipient-level tax entirely |

### Side by side

| Measure | Unplanned departure | Sequenced over 24 months |
|---|---|---|
| Covered expatriate status | Yes | Yes, unavoidable above $2 million |
| Gain crystallizing at expatriation | $4,000,000 | $2,800,000 |
| Exclusion applied | $910,000 | $910,000 |
| Taxable deemed sale gain | $3,090,000 | $1,890,000 |
| Tax on the deemed sale at 20% | $618,000 | $378,000 |
| Tax on retirement balances | $555,000 | $390,000 |
| Exposure for US-resident heirs | 40% on future transfers | Addressed before departure |
| **Total federal tax on departure** | **$1,173,000** | **$768,000** |
| **Difference** | | **$405,000** |

Covered status was never avoidable here, and pretending otherwise would be the mistake. Above $2 million of net worth, almost everyone reading this is covered. What planning changes is the size of the number and who ends up paying it.

*Assumptions: illustrative portfolio with harvestable losses available, retirement distributions spread across years with capacity below the top bracket, a 20% long-term capital gains rate with no net investment income tax on the deemed sale gain, and a 37% ordinary rate on retirement distributions. The $2 million net worth test is a cliff evaluated on a single date and is not indexed. Home country tax treatment, treaty relief, and foreign tax credits are not modeled and can change the result substantially in either direction. State tax is excluded.*

## Summary

| What we covered | The number |
|---|---|
| Estate tax exemption, US citizen or domiciliary | $15,000,000 |
| Estate tax exemption, non-resident non-domiciled | $60,000 |
| Annual gift limit to a non-citizen spouse | $194,000 |
| Covered expatriate net worth threshold | $2,000,000 |
| Exit tax exclusion, 2026 | $910,000 |
| Tax on gifts from a covered expatriate to a US recipient | 40% |

Three points carry the piece. Residence for income tax and domicile for estate tax are separate tests, and the second one carries a two hundred fifty to one difference in outcome. Leaving the US tax system is a taxable event that reaches retirement accounts, unrealized gains, and future gifts to family who stay behind. And in cross-border planning the entire advantage sits in the years before a status changes, which is why the conversation has to happen early.
