# Intelligent Wealth Series: Designing a Multi-Generational Investment Policy

## A Document That Decides Before You Have To

**For families whose capital now serves more than one household, more than one horizon, and more than one generation.**

Over the ten years ended December 2025, US funds and exchange-traded funds returned 9.9% annually in aggregate. The average dollar invested in those same funds earned 8.7%. The 1.2 point difference had nothing to do with the funds. It came entirely from when investors bought and sold.

That gap is roughly 12% of the total return, given up for free, by people who owned the right things at the wrong times. An investment policy statement is the least glamorous document in wealth management and the one specifically designed to close it. It is not a forecast. It is a set of decisions made calmly, in writing, that bind the version of you that will be reading headlines at the bottom of a drawdown.

## What the Document Actually Does

### The sections that matter

| Section | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| Purpose | What this capital is for, and who it serves |
| Governance | Who decides, who executes, and who reviews |
| Return objective | The rate required to sustain the purpose |
| Risk capacity and tolerance | What decline is survivable, and what is bearable |
| Asset allocation and ranges | Targets, and the bands that trigger action |
| Liquidity requirements | Cash needs by year, including capital calls |
| Distribution policy | How much comes out, on what formula |
| Tax management | Location, harvesting, and realization discipline |
| Rebalancing | The triggers and the process, agreed in advance |
| Restricted holdings | What this family will not own, and why |
| Review cadence | When it is read, and what permits an amendment |

### The arithmetic of lasting

| Component | Illustrative rate |
|---|---|
| Distribution to the family | 4.0% |
| Inflation | 2.5% |
| Investment and advisory fees | 0.6% |
| Tax drag on a taxable portfolio | 1.0% |
| **Required nominal return to preserve purchasing power** | **8.1%** |
| University endowment ten-year average return, through FY25 | 7.7% |

This is the table most families have never seen. A tax-exempt endowment needs to clear a lower bar than a taxable family pool spending the same percentage, and endowments only just cleared theirs over the past decade. The distribution rate is the single most powerful variable in the document.

Spending rates have been drifting upward under budget pressure. That is worth watching, because a distribution rate that rises during difficult years is precisely the behavior a policy exists to govern.

### The gap the policy is built to close

| Measure, ten years ended December 2025 | Figure |
|---|---|
| Aggregate annual total return of US funds and ETFs | 9.9% |
| Return earned by the average dollar invested in them | 8.7% |
| Annual gap | 1.2 percentage points |
| Share of the available return forgone | About 12% |

One encouraging note from the same research. In US stock funds specifically, the average dollar earned 12.8% against an aggregate 13.3%, meaning investors captured nearly all of it. The gap widens in complicated products and volatile ones. Simplicity is itself a behavioral defense.

### Why a family policy is not an institutional one

| Dimension | Endowment | Family |
|---|---|---|
| Tax status | Exempt | Taxable, at different rates in different accounts |
| Beneficiaries | One institution | Several people with different needs |
| Time horizon | Perpetual and uniform | Overlapping, from a few years to generations |
| Illiquid holdings | Chosen deliberately | Often inherited, and sometimes an operating business |
| Governance | A committee operating under a charter | Whoever happens to be in the room |
| Amendment process | Formal and minuted | Frequently a phone call |

### What a written policy gives you

- A distribution formula that does not renegotiate itself every year
- Rebalancing bands that force action in the direction nobody wants to go
- Manager evaluation criteria set before performance makes them emotional
- A return objective derived from the family's actual spending rather than from a benchmark
- A record of why the allocation looks the way it does, readable by people not yet in the room
- A required process for changing the policy, which is the provision that does the most work

### Why this matters now

Capital that served one household is now serving three. Beneficiaries have different ages, different tax situations, and different views. The concentration, private market, and liquidity questions covered earlier in this series all have to resolve into a single set of instructions that outlive the conversation that produced them.

The policy also becomes the institutional memory. In twenty years the people reading it will not have been in the meeting. What they will have is the document.

## What the Numbers Look Like in Practice

A composite family holds a $50 million pool supporting three branches. There is no written policy. Allocation decisions get made in conversations, distributions are negotiated annually, and managers are replaced after disappointing stretches.

### The cost of no action

Applying the documented behavior gap to their own capital.

| Measure over ten years | Amount |
|---|---|
| Starting portfolio | $50,000,000 |
| Value compounding at the aggregate fund return of 9.9% | $128,500,000 |
| Value compounding at the average investor return of 8.7% | $115,100,000 |
| **Difference** | **$13,400,000** |

Nothing in that gap is a fee, a tax, or a bad manager. It is the cost of deciding in the moment.

### The benefit of acting now

| Provision | What it prevents |
|---|---|
| Rebalancing bands with automatic triggers | Selling after declines and buying after run-ups |
| A distribution formula on a three-year trailing average | Spending that rises in good years and gets cut in bad ones |
| Manager termination criteria set in advance | Replacing managers at the bottom of their cycle |
| A mandatory waiting period before any policy amendment | Decisions made during the week that felt urgent |
| Defined roles and a written amendment process | The loudest voice in the room setting the allocation |
| A liquidity schedule covering capital calls and taxes | Forced sales at the worst available prices |

### Side by side

| Measure | No written policy | Written policy, followed |
|---|---|---|
| Portfolio today | $50,000,000 | $50,000,000 |
| Distribution policy | Renegotiated each year | Formula on a trailing average |
| Rebalancing | When someone notices | At defined bands |
| Manager changes | After poor performance | Against pre-set criteria |
| Ten-year annualized result | 8.7% | 9.9% |
| Value at ten years | $115,100,000 | $128,500,000 |
| **Difference** | | **$13,400,000** |

### What this comparison assumes

The behavior gap is a population average across millions of investors, and applying it to one family is illustrative rather than predictive. A written policy does not guarantee that a family captures the full return, and some families behave well with nothing written down.

What the research does establish is that the gap is real, that it is behavioral rather than structural, and that it is largest in exactly the complicated and volatile holdings that sophisticated families tend to own. A policy is not a promise of better returns. It is a commitment device, and its value is precisely equal to the number of times it is followed when someone would rather not.

Three points carry the piece. The distribution rate is the most consequential number in the document, and a taxable family pool needs a higher return than a tax-exempt endowment to sustain the same spending. The gap between what investments return and what investors earn is behavioral, which means it is addressable by process rather than by selection. And the provision that does the most work is the one governing how the policy itself may be changed.

### What that looks like in practice

- Deriving the return objective from your actual distribution needs, inflation, fees, and tax drag rather than from a benchmark
- Writing rebalancing bands and manager criteria that are specific enough to be uncomfortable to ignore
- Building a liquidity schedule into the policy so capital calls and tax payments are anticipated rather than absorbed
- Reviewing the policy on a set cadence with the next generation present, so it is understood before it is inherited
- Requiring a documented process for amendment, which is what separates a policy from a preference

### The questions worth asking

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| What return do we actually need, and where did that number come from? | Most objectives are borrowed rather than calculated |
| What is our distribution rate, and does it survive a bad decade? | It is the most powerful variable in the document |
| What triggers a rebalance, and who executes it? | If the answer is judgment, it will not happen |
| Under what conditions would we fire a manager? | Decide before performance makes it emotional |
| How do we change this policy? | The amendment process is the policy's real backbone |

The document is not the point. The point is that the decisions were made when everyone was calm, by people who wrote down why. Ask your Farther advisor to draft yours before the next market gives you a reason to need it.
