## What Governance Actually Means

### A word about the statistic you have heard

| Claim | Origin | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 70% of wealth transfers fail by the second generation, 90% by the third | Williams and Preisser, research into 3,250 families, published 2003 | The most quoted figure in this field |
| That 70% figure traces back to a business continuity rate rather than a study of wealth transitions | Published critique in a family office journal, 2022 | Every citation traced to its original source |

We include the challenge because the number deserves it. What survives the scrutiny better than the headline is the causes analysis, and the causes analysis is the part that is actually useful.

### Where transitions actually break down

| Cause of failed transitions | Share |
|---|---|
| Breakdown of communication and trust within the family | 60% |
| Heirs inadequately prepared for the responsibility | 25% |
| Everything else, including tax, legal, investment, and documents | 15% |

*Reported breakdowns vary somewhat across published versions of this research.*

The wealth management industry is organized almost entirely around the bottom row. That is where the expertise is, where the fees are, and where the conversation is comfortable.

### The disclosure gap

| Among households with $3 million or more in investable assets | Share |
|---|---|
| Have disclosed little or nothing about their wealth to their children | 64% |

The usual reasons are reasonable ones. Parents worry about removing motivation, about children being taken advantage of, about a number becoming an expectation. Those concerns are legitimate. Silence is not the only response to them.

### The structures, and what each one is for

| Structure | What it handles | Who participates |
|---|---|---|
| Family assembly | Connection, information, shared history | All adult family members |
| Family council | Policy, education, communication, conflict process | Elected or appointed representatives |
| Board or investment committee | Capital allocation and oversight | Family members plus independent members |
| Family charter | The rules everything else operates under | Ratified by the family |
| Trustee and successor structure | Distributions and fiduciary judgment | Named individuals or a corporate trustee |

Small families do not need all of this. Most need a charter, a regular meeting, and a written answer to who decides what.

### The questions that need an owner before they need an answer

| Decision | Who decides it today? |
|---|---|
| Who may work in the family business, and on what terms | |
| How and when distributions are made, and on what basis | |
| Whether a family member may sell an interest, and to whom | |
| How disagreements are resolved without going to court | |
| Who selects, monitors, and replaces the trustee | |
| What information each generation receives, and at what age | |

If the right column is blank or reads "Dad," the family has a plan for its assets and no plan for itself.

### What a governance structure gives you

- A written process for disagreement, established while everyone still agrees
- Distribution policy set by principle rather than negotiated case by case under pressure
- The next generation learning judgment on small decisions before they inherit large ones
- Roles in the business filled by qualification rather than by birth order
- Information shared deliberately, on a schedule, instead of discovered at a funeral
- A family that knows what the money is for, which is the only durable protection there is

### Why this matters now

A very large amount of wealth is about to change hands, with projections of well over one hundred trillion dollars transferring through 2048 and roughly half of that coming from households in this segment. Volume alone guarantees that a great many families will be running this experiment at the same time.

The families who do well will not be the ones with better documents. Almost everyone at this level has good documents. They will be the ones who started the conversation ten years before it was necessary, when the stakes of a difficult discussion were still low.

## What the Numbers Look Like in Practice

A composite family holds $60 million, including a $35 million operating business. There are three adult children. One works in the business and expects to lead it. The other two have never been told what the business is worth or what the estate plan says.

### The cost of no action

The founder dies without a written succession policy or a process for disagreement.

| Consequence | Estimated cost |
|---|---|
| Legal and professional fees across a contested three-year settlement | $1,500,000 |
| Discount on a forced business sale at 20% of enterprise value | $7,000,000 |
| Estate tax assessed on appraised value while proceeds arrive lower | Additional |
| Operating disruption during the dispute | Not modeled |
| **Quantifiable cost** | **$8,500,000** |
| Relationships among three siblings | Not recoverable |

The estate plan worked exactly as drafted. The documents were not the problem.

### The benefit of acting now

A governance program built over the decade before the transition.

| Component | Cost |
|---|---|
| Facilitated family meetings, twice yearly | $40,000 per year |
| Family charter drafted and periodically revised | $50,000, one time |
| Next generation education and involvement program | $25,000 per year |
| Two independent board members | $60,000 per year |
| **Ten-year total** | **Approximately $1,300,000** |

### Side by side

| Measure | No governance structure | Governance built in advance |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of the structure over ten years | $0 | $1,300,000 |
| Legal and professional fees at transition | $1,500,000 | Substantially lower |
| Discount on a forced sale | $7,000,000 | Avoided |
| Succession decision | Made by a court or a clock | Made by the family, in advance |
| Siblings informed | At the reading | For a decade |
| **Quantifiable outcome** | **$8,500,000 cost** | **$1,300,000 cost** |

### What this table cannot claim

Governance does not guarantee a good outcome. Families with excellent structures still have painful disagreements, and some families transition beautifully with nothing written down at all. What a structure changes is the odds, and what it provides is a process that exists before the disagreement does.

The honest case is not that governance prevents conflict. It is that conflict arrives in every family eventually, and it is far cheaper to decide how you will handle it while nobody is angry.

*Assumptions: illustrative professional fees, an assumed 20% discount on a business sold under duress, and illustrative governance program costs that vary widely by family size, complexity, and provider. No probability is assigned to a contested transition, and no claim is made that governance prevents one. Costs and outcomes will differ materially by family.*

## Summary

| Share of failed transitions attributed to communication and trust | 60% |
| Share attributed to unprepared heirs | 25% |
| Share attributed to tax, legal, investment, and documents combined | 15% |

Three points carry the piece. The technical work that consumes almost all of the planning conversation accounts for a small share of what actually goes wrong. The most reliable predictor of a successful transition is whether the family talks to each other, and most families at this level do not. And governance is not a document, it is a set of answers to who decides what, written down while everyone is still on speaking terms.
