## Absolute Return, Explained Plainly

### A different objective, not a better one

Traditional managers are measured against an index. Absolute return managers aim to make money regardless of market direction, which means the product you are buying is not return. It is return that behaves differently from everything else you own. Judged as a return engine, these strategies will usually disappoint. Judged as a shock absorber, some of them earn their place.

### The strategies behind the label

| Strategy | What it does | What it needs to work |
|---|---|---|
| Equity long/short | Owns winners, sells losers short | Wide dispersion between good and bad companies |
| Global macro | Directional positions in rates, currencies, commodities | Policy and economic divergence |
| Event driven | Mergers, restructurings, spin-offs | Steady deal flow and completions |
| Relative value | Exploits pricing gaps between related securities | Volatility and mispricing |
| Multi-strategy | Many independent teams under centralized risk control | Scale, talent, and capital |

### The long record versus the recent one

| Period | Average annual return | Alpha |
|---|---|---|
| The 2010s | Roughly 4% | Roughly 50 basis points |
| Five-year annualized, above cash | 7.96% | 466 basis points over cash |
| 2025 | 11.2% | Positive across every strategy |

The industry saw roughly $30 billion of annual outflows from 2016 through 2023. The turnaround is real and recent. Two strong years are not a track record on their own.

### What you actually pay

| Fee structure | Effective annual cost of assets |
|---|---|
| Traditional headline terms | 2% management plus 20% of gains |
| Multi-strategy with pass-through expenses | 3% to 10% |

Pass-through structures charge investors directly for compensation, technology, research, and operating costs. The headline number on the first page of the deck is often less than half of what the position actually costs.

### The number nobody puts on the slide

Most hedge fund gains are short-term and taxed as ordinary income. Long-term equity gains are not taxed until realized, and appreciation held until death currently receives a basis adjustment.

| $10 million earning 11% gross | Absolute return strategy | Tax-efficient equity |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-tax gain | $1,100,000 | $1,100,000 |
| Portion currently taxable | $1,100,000 | $200,000 |
| Federal tax | $448,800 at 40.8% | $47,600 at 23.8% |
| **After-tax gain** | **$651,200** | **$1,052,400** |
| **After-tax return** | **6.5%** | **10.5%** |

*Assumes a 37% top federal ordinary rate plus 3.8% net investment income tax, a 20% long-term capital gains rate plus 3.8%, a 2% dividend yield on the equity position, and appreciation held rather than realized. State tax is not included and would widen the gap in most states.*

### What a disciplined absolute return allocation gives you

- Return that does not depend on markets rising, which is the entire point of the category
- Drawdown mitigation when equity concentration is already high elsewhere on your balance sheet
- Access to strategies unavailable in long-only form, including relative value and true market-neutral
- A negotiated, fully disclosed fee including pass-through expenses rather than a headline rate
- Placement in the account type where the tax treatment does the least damage
- A defined role, so the position is judged on the job it was hired for

### Why this matters now

Two conditions make the category more relevant than it was five years ago. Index concentration sits at multi-decade highs, and the stock-bond correlation that made fixed income reliable ballast has been inconsistent. Both are arguments for owning something that behaves differently.

One condition cuts the other way. Reported correlation between hedge fund returns and global equities reached roughly 0.92 in 2025 by one prime broker's measure, the highest in at least five years. If the diversification is the product, that is the number to watch.

*Return, fee, and correlation figures reflect third-party research current through mid-2026 and vary by provider and index methodology. Reported industry averages differ by source, with 2025 figures ranging from roughly 10.5% to 12.5%.*

## What the Numbers Look Like in Practice

A composite household is offered a $10 million allocation to a well-regarded multi-strategy fund, to be held in a taxable account. The question is not whether the manager is good. The question is what the manager has to deliver.

### The hurdle, built from the bottom up

| Step | Rate |
|---|---|
| After-tax return the family already earns on tax-efficient equity | 10.5% |
| Grossed up for ordinary income tax at 40.8% | 17.7% |
| Plus effective fee load of 4% | 21.7% |
| Average hedge fund return in a strong year | 11.2% |

To match what the family already owns, the fund needs to gross roughly 21.7% before fees, every year, in a taxable account. The industry's best calendar year since 2009 was half that.

### The cost of no action

| On a $10 million taxable allocation | Amount |
|---|---|
| Assumed gross return | $1,100,000 |
| Effective fees at 4% | $400,000 |
| Ordinary income tax at 40.8% on the remainder | $285,600 |
| **Net to the family** | **$414,400** |
| **Net return** | **4.1%** |

The manager did fine. The structure did not.

### The benefit of acting deliberately

| Action | Effect |
|---|---|
| Move the allocation into tax-deferred capacity where it exists | Removes the ordinary income drag entirely |
| Reduce the taxable-account allocation to $4 million | Sizes the position to the role it plays rather than the return it promises |
| Require full fee disclosure including pass-through expenses | Brings the negotiated all-in cost from 4% toward 2.5% |
| Select for measured correlation rather than headline return | Buys the diversification you are actually paying for |

### Side by side

| Measure | As offered | As structured |
|---|---|---|
| Allocation in taxable account | $10.0M | $4.0M |
| Allocation in tax-deferred capacity | $0 | $3.0M |
| Redeployed to tax-efficient equity | $0 | $3.0M |
| Effective fee load | 4.0% | 2.5% |
| Annual fee paid | $400,000 | $175,000 |
| Required gross return to justify the taxable portion | 21.7% | 15.3% |
| Portfolio role | Undefined | Drawdown mitigation |

The family kept absolute return exposure. It stopped paying ordinary income rates on the majority of it, cut the annual fee by $225,000, and lowered the bar the manager has to clear from implausible to achievable.

*Assumptions: hypothetical 11% gross return, 40.8% combined federal ordinary rate, illustrative fee loads, and available tax-deferred capacity that many households will not have. State tax is excluded. Fee terms, tax treatment, and account eligibility vary materially by investor and vehicle. Lower fees do not imply higher returns, and no strategy prevents loss.*

## Summary

| What we covered | The number |
|---|---|
| Hedge fund industry assets | Over $5 trillion |
| Average return, the 2010s | Roughly 4% |
| Average return, 2025 | 11.2% |
| Effective fees on pass-through multi-strategy funds | 3% to 10% |
| After-tax return on 11% gross, taxable account | 6.5% |
| Gross return required to match tax-efficient equity | 21.7% |

Three points carry the piece. Absolute return strategies sell behavior, not performance, so the correlation number matters more than the return number. Headline fees and actual fees are different things, sometimes by a factor of three. And in a taxable account, ordinary income treatment sets a hurdle so high that where you hold the position matters more than which manager you pick.
