# Intelligent Wealth Series: Liquidity Engineering for UHNW Portfolios

## Wealth Is Not the Same Thing as Access to Cash

**For families whose balance sheet is large, illiquid, and occasionally asked to produce money on somebody else's schedule.**

Most liquidity problems at this level are not solvency problems. They are timing problems. A capital call arrives on ten days' notice. An estate tax bill is due in cash nine months after a death. A tax payment lands in a quarter when markets are down.

None of those events care what your net worth is. They care what you can convert to cash this month without destroying something. The federal funds target range sits at 3.50% to 3.75%, which makes borrowed liquidity meaningfully more expensive than it was three years ago, while distributions from private funds have slowed. Those two facts together are why this deserves engineering rather than improvisation.

## Building the Capacity Before You Need It

### Where the demands come from

| Liquidity event | Timing you control | Typical size |
|---|---|---|
| Federal estate tax | None, due nine months after death in cash | Up to 40% of the amount above exemption |
| Private markets capital call | None, often ten days' notice | Whatever remains uncalled |
| Tax on a realized gain | Partial, quarterly and April | 23.8% to 40.8% of the gain |
| Option exercise before expiration | None, fixed expiration | Strike price plus the tax |
| Business working capital need | Rarely | Variable |
| An opportunity that will not wait | None | Variable |

### The cost of capital ladder

| Source | Typical rate |
|---|---|
| Existing primary residence mortgage | 3.4% |
| Investment property mortgage | 4.5% |
| Portfolio line of credit | 5.2% |
| Home equity line of credit | 6.1% |
| Federal funds target range, August 2026 | 3.50% to 3.75% |

### The asset most families forget they own

| Measure among high-net-worth households | Figure |
|---|---|
| Average primary residence mortgage rate | 3.4% |
| Share with a mortgage rate below 4% | 73% |
| Share in the 2% to 3% range | 48% |
| Share owning the primary residence outright | One third |
| Debt-to-assets ratio above $25 million of net worth | 4% |

A mortgage locked at 3% is a financial asset. Refinancing it to raise cash retires that asset and replaces it with debt at current rates. It is often the most expensive way to solve a short-term problem, and it is frequently the first idea suggested.

### The liquidity ladder

| Source | Speed | Cost | What it puts at risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash reserve | Immediate | Opportunity cost | Nothing |
| Sale of liquid securities | Days | Capital gains tax, and timing | Realizes gains, or losses if forced |
| Portfolio line of credit | Days once established | Around 5% | Collateral call in a drawdown |
| Existing home equity line | Weeks | Around 6% | The residence |
| New mortgage or refinance | Weeks to months | Current market rates | Retires cheap existing debt |
| Life insurance held in trust | At death, immediately | Premiums paid over years | Nothing, if funded early |
| Secondary sale of fund interests | Months | Discount to stated value | Crystallizes a discount |
| Sale of the business or property | Months to years | Full price impact | Forced timing |

### What deliberate liquidity engineering gives you

- Credit facilities arranged while your balance sheet looks strong rather than while it is under stress
- A cash reserve sized to known obligations over the next twenty-four months rather than to a rule of thumb
- Capital call schedules modeled against tax payments and other commitments on one calendar
- Estate tax liquidity solved with insurance or deferral rather than a forced sale of the business
- Cheap existing debt preserved instead of refinanced away
- The ability to be a buyer during a drawdown rather than a seller

### Why this matters now

Three conditions are stacked. Borrowing costs more than it did during the period when most family balance sheets were built. Private fund distributions have slowed materially, so capital committed years ago is still working while new calls continue to arrive. And a large share of households carry mortgage debt at rates they will never see again, which makes the usual answer of refinancing genuinely costly.

The families who handle this well are not the ones with the most cash. They are the ones who arranged access before they needed it, when arranging it was easy.

## What the Numbers Look Like in Practice

A composite family holds $30 million across a business, a liquid portfolio, private fund interests, and a home. In a single quarter a $1.5 million capital call and a $900,000 tax payment arrive together, during a 20% market decline.

### The cost of no action

No facility in place, so the portfolio becomes the source.

| Measure | Amount |
|---|---|
| Cash required within 60 days | $2,400,000 |
| Cash on hand | $200,000 |
| Securities sold to cover the balance | $2,200,000 |
| Value of those same shares before the decline | $2,750,000 |
| **Permanent cost of selling into the drawdown** | **$550,000** |

The tax bill and the capital call both got paid. The family funded them by selling assets at the worst available price and will not participate in the recovery on that portion.

### The benefit of acting now

| Action | Effect |
|---|---|
| Establish a portfolio line of credit while markets are calm | Provides $2.2 million without selling a share |
| Size the cash reserve to known obligations twenty-four months out | Covers the tax payment outright |
| Map capital call schedules against the tax calendar | Turns a surprise into a scheduled item |
| Leave the 3.4% mortgage untouched | Preserves debt that cannot be replaced |

### Side by side

| Measure | No facility in place | Liquidity arranged in advance |
|---|---|---|
| Cash required | $2,400,000 | $2,400,000 |
| Securities sold | $2,200,000 | $0 |
| Value permanently forfeited | $550,000 | $0 |
| Interest cost over twelve months at 5.2% | $0 | $114,400 |
| Cash reserve applied | $200,000 | $900,000 |
| Portfolio positioned for the recovery | No | Yes |
| **Net difference** | | **$435,600** |

The family paid $114,400 of interest to avoid $550,000 of permanent loss. That is what liquidity is actually for.

### The risk this table does not show

A portfolio line of credit is secured by the portfolio, and lenders reduce advance rates and issue collateral calls precisely when markets fall. A facility that looked ample at the start of a decline can shrink partway through it. Borrowing also leaves the market exposure fully in place, so a family that borrows against a falling portfolio owns both the decline and the loan.

The answer is not to avoid credit. It is to size the facility well below its maximum, keep genuine cash alongside it, and never let a credit line be the only plan.

*Assumptions: illustrative portfolio values, a 20% market decline with a subsequent full recovery, a 5.2% borrowing rate held for twelve months, and continuous availability of the credit facility. Advance rates, collateral requirements, and rates on securities-based lending vary by lender and can change without notice. Interest on borrowing may or may not be deductible depending on use and circumstances. State tax is excluded.*

Three points carry the piece. Liquidity events arrive on schedules you do not set, which means capacity has to exist before the need does. The cheapest liquidity most families own is the low-rate debt already on their balance sheet, and refinancing it away to raise cash is usually the worst available trade. And credit facilities are least reliable exactly when they are most needed, so a line of credit belongs alongside real cash rather than in place of it.

### The questions worth asking

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| How much uncalled capital do I have committed? | It is an obligation, not an investment |
| What would I sell if I needed $2 million in thirty days? | The answer reveals the plan, or the absence of one |
| Do I have a facility arranged, and at what advance rate? | Arranging one under stress is expensive or impossible |
| Where would the cash for estate tax come from? | Nine months is not long enough to sell a business |
| What is the rate on my existing mortgage? | It may be the best asset on the page |

The point of liquidity is not to hold cash. It is to never be the person who has to sell. Ask your Farther advisor to build your obligation calendar before the next call arrives.
