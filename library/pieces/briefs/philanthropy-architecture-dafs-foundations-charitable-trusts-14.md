# Intelligent Wealth Series: Philanthropy Architecture: DAFs, Foundations, and Charitable Trusts

## Give the Same Amount, Keep More of the Benefit

**For families whose giving is already meaningful and who want the structure behind it to work as hard as the intent.**

On January 1, 2026, the rules for charitable deductions changed more than they have in decades. Itemizers now lose the first 0.5% of adjusted gross income before any charitable deduction applies. Top-bracket donors see the value of that deduction capped at 35% rather than their 37% marginal rate. Both provisions are permanent.

For a household with $1 million of income giving $100,000, the same gift that produced a $37,000 federal benefit in 2025 produces $33,250 in 2026. Nothing about the generosity changed. The architecture around it now matters more than it did, and the tools to respond already exist.

## Structure Before Amount

### What changed this year

| Provision | Through 2025 | Starting 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Floor on itemized charitable deductions | None | 0.5% of adjusted gross income |
| Value of the deduction, top-bracket donors | 37% | 35% |
| Corporate charitable floor | None | 1% of taxable income |
| Cash gift limit to public charities | 60%, set to revert to 50% | 60%, permanent |
| Deduction for non-itemizers | None | $1,000 single, $2,000 joint |
| Qualified charitable distribution limit | $108,000 in 2025 | $111,000 in 2026 |

*The non-itemizer deduction does not apply to contributions to donor advised funds.*

### What the floor costs at different income levels

| Adjusted gross income | Non-deductible first dollars |
|---|---|
| $500,000 | $2,500 |
| $1,000,000 | $5,000 |
| $2,500,000 | $12,500 |
| $5,000,000 | $25,000 |
| $10,000,000 | $50,000 |

The floor applies every year. Give the same total across five years instead of one and you lose it five times.

### The two changes together

| Measure | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Adjusted gross income | $1,000,000 | $1,000,000 |
| Charitable contributions | $100,000 | $100,000 |
| Non-deductible floor | $0 | $5,000 |
| Deductible amount | $100,000 | $95,000 |
| Rate applied to the deduction | 37% | 35% |
| **Federal tax benefit** | **$37,000** | **$33,250** |
| **Reduction** | | **About 10%** |

### The three main vehicles

| | Donor advised fund | Private foundation | Charitable remainder trust |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deduction limit, cash | 60% of AGI | 30% of AGI | Partial, based on remainder value |
| Deduction limit, appreciated securities | 30% of AGI | 20% of AGI | Partial |
| Annual distribution requirement | None | 5% of assets | Pays the donor first |
| Excise tax on investment income | None | 1.39% | None |
| Public disclosure | None | Annual return is public | None |
| Donor control | Advisory recommendations | Full, subject to rules | Governed by trust terms |
| Can compensate family members | No | Yes, within limits | No |
| Setup and ongoing cost | Low | Substantial | Moderate |

A donor advised fund gives the better deduction and the lower cost. A private foundation gives control, permanence, and a governance structure the next generation can participate in. Many families use both, and the choice is usually about what the family wants to build rather than what the deduction is worth.

### The lever that helps twice

Qualified charitable distributions from an IRA are not deductions. They come out of adjusted gross income entirely, which means they never touch the 0.5% floor and they also lower the AGI that the floor, the deduction cap, the net investment income tax, and other thresholds are all measured against. For donors over 70 and a half, the limit is $111,000 in 2026.

### What a considered philanthropy structure gives you

- Gifts timed so the annual floor applies once rather than repeatedly
- Appreciated securities donated directly, avoiding capital gains entirely on the embedded appreciation
- Deduction limits used deliberately, since the cash and appreciated-property caps differ by vehicle
- A giving vehicle matched to whether you want simplicity or a family institution
- Distributions from retirement accounts routed to reduce income rather than to create a deduction
- A governance framework the next generation can learn inside of

### Why this matters now

Two things are true at once. The tax benefit of ordinary annual giving fell in 2026 and will stay lower. And the structural responses that recover most of that value, principally bunching and donating appreciated property rather than cash, were always available and are now worth substantially more than they were.

The families who change nothing will give the same amount and keep less. The families who restructure will give the same amount, or more, and keep considerably more than they did last year.

## What the Numbers Look Like in Practice

A composite family has $3 million of adjusted gross income, gives roughly $200,000 a year in cash, and holds a long-term equity position worth $1 million with a $200,000 cost basis.

### The cost of no action

Five more years of writing checks.

| Measure | Per year | Over five years |
|---|---|---|
| Cash given | $200,000 | $1,000,000 |
| Non-deductible floor at 0.5% of AGI | $15,000 | $75,000 |
| Deductible amount | $185,000 | $925,000 |
| Federal benefit at 35% | $64,750 | $323,750 |
| Capital gains avoided | $0 | $0 |

### The benefit of acting now

The family contributes the appreciated position to a donor advised fund in a single year and grants from it over the following five, keeping the charities whole on the same schedule.

| Measure | Amount |
|---|---|
| Value contributed | $1,000,000 |
| Cost basis | $200,000 |
| Embedded gain never realized | $800,000 |
| Capital gains tax avoided at 23.8% | $190,400 |
| Floor applied once rather than five times | $15,000 instead of $75,000 |
| Deductible amount | $985,000 |
| Deductible in year one, capped at 30% of AGI | $900,000 |
| Carried forward to later years | $85,000 |

### Side by side

| Measure | Annual cash gifts | Bunched fund, appreciated stock |
|---|---|---|
| Total given to charity | $1,000,000 | $1,000,000 |
| Timing of grants to charities | Annual | Annual |
| Times the 0.5% floor applies | 5 | 1 |
| Deduction lost to the floor | $75,000 | $15,000 |
| Total deductible | $925,000 | $985,000 |
| Federal deduction benefit at 35% | $323,750 | $344,750 |
| Capital gains tax avoided | $0 | $190,400 |
| **Total federal benefit** | **$323,750** | **$535,150** |
| **Difference** | | **$211,400** |

The charities receive the same money on the same schedule. The family gave away an asset it was never going to sell without paying tax on, cleared the floor once instead of five times, and kept an additional $211,400.

*Assumptions: $3 million AGI held constant, a 35% effective deduction rate under the top-bracket cap, a 23.8% combined long-term capital gains rate, a 30% of AGI limitation on gifts of appreciated property to public charities with a five-year carryforward, and sufficient other income to absorb the carryforward. Deduction limits, carryforward ordering, and substantiation rules are more complex than shown. Appreciated property gifts require a qualified appraisal in certain circumstances. State tax is excluded.*

## Summary

| What we covered | The number |
|---|---|
| New floor on itemized charitable deductions | 0.5% of AGI |
| Cap on deduction value for top-bracket donors | 35% |
| Reduction in benefit on an unchanged $100,000 gift | About 10% |
| Deduction limit, appreciated securities to a donor advised fund | 30% of AGI |
| Private foundation annual distribution requirement | 5% |
| Illustration: additional benefit from restructuring | $211,400 |

Three points carry the piece. The 2026 rules reduced the tax benefit of routine annual giving, permanently, and the reduction grows with income. Bunching several years of gifts into one clears the annual floor once instead of repeatedly, which is now worth real money on its own. And donating appreciated securities rather than cash remains the single largest unclaimed benefit in charitable planning, because it removes a capital gain that would otherwise be paid eventually.
