**For sophisticated investors who want to know what the model is actually telling them, and what it cannot see.**

Modern Portfolio Theory earned Harry Markowitz a Nobel Prize and still sits underneath nearly every allocation recommendation you will ever receive. It answers a real question well: for a given level of risk, which mix produces the most return.

The catch is that the answer is only as good as three inputs, and for a household holding private funds, an operating business, and a taxable account, at least two of those inputs arrive distorted. Recent research found that correcting for one distortion alone doubles the measured volatility of buyout funds. Knowing where the model is strong and where it is blind is what separates a portfolio that was built from one that was accepted.

## How the Model Works, and What It Assumes

### The three inputs

Every efficient frontier is generated from three things: an expected return for each asset class, a volatility figure for each, and the correlations between them. Change any one and the recommended portfolio changes with it. The output looks precise. The inputs are estimates.

| Input | What the model requires | What a household actually has |
|---|---|---|
| Expected return | A forward estimate per asset class | Estimates revised annually, with wide error bars |
| Volatility | Observed variation in price | Quarterly appraisals for much of the balance sheet |
| Correlation | Stable relationships between assets | Relationships that converge in the drawdowns that matter most |

### The smoothing problem

Public securities reprice every second. Private funds are typically valued quarterly, often using models and comparable transactions rather than transactions in the asset itself. The result is a return series that looks calmer than the underlying business. Cliff Asness named this volatility laundering. Researchers correct for it by adjusting for the serial correlation that appraisal-based valuations introduce.

| Asset class | Reported volatility | Volatility after correction |
|---|---|---|
| Small buyout funds | 11% | 22% |
| Large buyout funds | 12% | 21% |
| Late-stage venture capital | 19% | 38% |
| Early-stage venture capital | 29% | 87% |
| S&P 500, for reference | 19% | 19% |

*Corrected figures reflect a 2024 lagged-beta study. Public equity volatility of roughly 19% is measured since 1926 and needs no correction.*

Feed the left column into the model and it will recommend more private exposure than the right column supports. That is not an argument against private markets. It is an argument for sizing them against their real risk.

### What the model leaves out entirely

| The model assumes | The reality for your household |
|---|---|
| Every asset can be traded | The largest holding often cannot be sold |
| Returns are pre-tax | Every rebalance carries a tax cost |
| One period, then the exercise ends | A multi-generational horizon with gifting and estate events along the way |
| Risk equals volatility | Risk is failing to fund what you have committed to |
| The investor owns only the portfolio | Human capital and business income dominate the early decades |

### What disciplined construction gives you

- Position sizes set against corrected volatility rather than reported volatility
- An illiquidity budget you chose deliberately, sized to your actual capacity to wait
- Diversifiers selected on measured behavior rather than on asset-class labels
- Rebalancing rules that price the tax cost before the trade, not after
- Correlation assumptions stress-tested for the scenario where they converge
- A frontier that treats your business and your human capital as positions, because they are

### Why this matters now

Private market structures are reaching individual investors faster than allocation frameworks have been rewritten to handle them. The reported statistics on those structures make them look better than they are on a risk-adjusted basis, and the models most advisors run accept those statistics without adjustment. Households that correct the inputs get a defensible portfolio. Households that do not get a portfolio that is riskier than the page says it is.

## What the Numbers Look Like in Practice

A composite household: business owners with a $30 million operating company and a $25 million liquid portfolio. Their previous firm ran an allocation model using reported private market statistics and recommended 40% of the liquid portfolio in private funds.

### The same model, two sets of inputs

| | Built on reported inputs | Built on corrected inputs |
|---|---|---|
| Private fund allocation | 40% | 22% |
| Dollar amount | $10.0M | $5.5M |
| Of which venture capital | $3.0M | $1.0M |
| Capital locked up 10 years or longer | $10.0M | $5.5M |
| Liquid capital available in a drawdown | $15.0M | $19.5M |

### The cost of no action

| Risk measure on the private sleeve | As reported | As it actually behaves |
|---|---|---|
| Annual volatility applied | 11% | 22% |
| Typical annual swing on $10.0M | $1.1M | $2.2M |
| Understated risk the household never priced | — | $1.1M per year |

The household did not take on too much private exposure because someone misled them. They took it on because the reported numbers said $10 million of buyout funds carried the risk of $10 million of investment-grade bonds. It does not.

### The benefit of acting now

| Action | Effect |
|---|---|
| Correct volatility inputs before sizing positions | Private sleeve falls from $10.0M to $5.5M, matched to real risk |
| Set an illiquidity budget against actual liquidity needs | $4.5M returns to capital that can be deployed inside 30 days |
| Keep meaningful diversifier exposure rather than cutting to zero | Retains the return and risk-adjusted benefit the research supports |
| Sequence rebalancing against harvested losses | Reduces the tax cost of getting from here to there |

| Measure | Before | After |

Three points carry the piece. The efficient frontier is a conclusion drawn from three estimates, so the quality of the estimates determines the quality of the portfolio. Private market statistics arrive understated by construction, which biases models toward holding more of them. And the model was never built to see taxes, illiquidity, or the business that funds your life, which means those have to be added by the people running it.
