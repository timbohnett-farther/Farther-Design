# Intelligent Wealth Series: Private Equity, Private Credit, and Co-Investments

## Access Is Not the Same as Advantage

**For investors who can now reach private markets and want to know what separates a good allocation from an expensive one.**

Private markets used to be gated by minimums and relationships. That gate has largely opened. What has not changed is that outcomes here depend on manager selection to a degree that has no equivalent in public markets. Between the 25th and 75th percentile, large-cap equity managers differ by 2.6 percentage points. In venture capital, the gap between top and bottom quartile exceeds 30 points in most vintage years.

Meanwhile the cash is coming back more slowly than it used to, and private credit is facing its first serious test as an asset class. Both facts argue for participating deliberately rather than not at all.

## Three Different Things in One Category

### What you are actually buying

| | Private equity | Private credit | Co-investment |
|---|---|---|---|
| What you own | Equity in private companies | Loans to private companies | A direct stake alongside a sponsor |
| Where return comes from | Growth and exit price | Contracted interest | The underlying deal |
| Typical horizon | 10 to 12 years | 5 to 7 years | Tied to the deal |
| Cash flow shape | J-curve, distributions late | Current income from year one | Deal-dependent |
| Fee load | Management fee plus carry | Management fee plus incentive | Often reduced or waived |
| Primary risk | Manager selection and illiquidity | Credit loss and valuation opacity | Concentration and adverse selection |

### Why manager selection dominates

| Asset class | Spread between 25th and 75th percentile managers |
|---|---|
| Fixed income | 1.5 points |
| Large-cap public equity | 2.6 points |
| Buyout, 2022 vintage | Over 13 points |
| Venture capital, most vintages | Over 30 points |

In public markets, picking the wrong manager costs you a rounding error. In private markets it can cost you the entire premium you came for. Top-quartile buyout managers delivered roughly 22% IRR over the past decade against a median near 13%.

### What actually comes back, and when

| Cash returned per dollar invested by year eight | Multiple |
|---|---|
| Buyout, what investors typically expect | 1.5x |
| Private equity, recent average | 1.3x |
| Venture capital, 2010s average | 1.3x |
| Venture capital, today | 0.7x |

Distributions matter more than paper value. A fund can report an attractive multiple while returning very little cash, which is why institutional investors now weight realized distributions as heavily as total value. A meaningful share of 2021 and 2022 commitments has neither been called nor returned.

### Private credit, honestly stated

| Measure | 2026 reading |
|---|---|
| Market size | Roughly $2 trillion |
| Projected size by 2030 | $4 trillion to $5 trillion |
| First lien direct lending yield | 8.0% to 8.5% |
| Reported default rate, across published sources | 1.5% to 9.2% |

That last row is not a typo. Different providers count defaults differently, some excluding distressed exchanges and restructurings that are economically similar to a default. The disagreement is itself the disclosure. Regulators and large bank lenders raised questions about valuation practices in this market during 2026.

### What a disciplined private allocation gives you

- Exposure to companies that never reach public markets, where the projected long-run return for private equity is 10.2% annually
- Contracted income from private credit that floats with rates rather than depending on an exit
- Fee reduction through co-investment, which typically carries reduced or no management fee and carry
- Vintage diversification, so you are not making a single bet on one year's entry prices
- A capital call schedule you can plan around rather than react to
- Access chosen on manager quality rather than on what a distribution platform happens to sell

### Why this matters now

Distributions have slowed, which means capital committed years ago is still working and new commitments compete with it for liquidity. Private credit is being tested for the first time at scale. Neither is a reason to avoid the category. Adding roughly 30% diversified alternatives to a traditional 60/40 has been projected to lift long-run return from 6.4% to 6.9% and improve risk-adjusted return by about 25%. It is a reason to size, pace, and select with care.

## What the Numbers Look Like in Practice

A composite household with a $30 million liquid portfolio decides to build a $6 million private markets allocation. Two ways to do it.

### The cost of no action

| | Committed all at once | Paced and structured |
|---|---|---|
| Vintage years covered | 1 | 3 |
| Strategies | 1 | 3 |
| Managers | 2 | 6 |
| Share of allocation exposed to one year's entry prices | 100% | 33% |
| Capital call timing | Reactive | Modeled three years out |

Committing the full amount in a single year is not a small decision. Entry multiples in one vintage drive returns for a decade, and the 2022 vintage alone produced a spread of more than 13 points between the best and worst buyout managers.

### Where the fees go

| Fee component over a ten-year life | Committed all at once | Paced and structured |
|---|---|---|
| Fund management fees | $900,000 | $630,000 |
| Platform or access fees | $300,000 | $0 |
| **Total fee load** | **$1,200,000** | **$630,000** |
| **Difference** | | **$570,000** |

### The benefit of acting deliberately

| Action | Effect |
|---|---|
| Pace $2 million per year across three vintages | Removes single-year entry price risk |
| Allocate 30% of the equity sleeve to co-investments | Eliminates management fee and carry on $1.8 million |
| Add private credit alongside private equity | Introduces current income against a ten-year J-curve |
| Access managers directly rather than through a platform layer | Removes $300,000 of access fees over the fund life |

### Side by side

| Measure | Committed all at once | Paced and structured |
|---|---|---|
| Total committed | $6.0M | $6.0M |
| Vintage years | 1 | 3 |
| Strategies represented | 1 | 3 |
| Ten-year fee load | $1.2M | $630K |
| Capital freed by lower fees | — | $570K |
| Income received during the J-curve years | Minimal | From the credit sleeve |
| Exposure to one manager's outcome | High | Spread across six |

The family invested the same $6 million. What changed is how many ways it can go right.

*Assumptions: illustrative fee load of approximately 1.5% annually averaged across a ten-year fund life on fund-committed capital, a 0.5% annual platform access fee in the first scenario, and no management fee or carry on the co-investment sleeve. Carried interest is not modeled and would apply to profitable fund investments in both scenarios. Actual terms vary materially by manager and vehicle. Lower fees do not imply higher returns.*

## Summary

| What we covered | The number |
|---|---|
| Manager dispersion, large-cap public equity | 2.6 points |
| Manager dispersion, venture capital | Over 30 points |
| Venture capital cash returned by year eight, then versus now | 1.3x vs 0.7x |
| First lien direct lending yield, 2026 | 8.0% to 8.5% |
| Published private credit default rates, range | 1.5% to 9.2% |
| Projected long-run private equity return | 10.2% |

Three points carry the piece. Private markets are not one asset class but three distinct exposures with different cash flow shapes and different risks. Manager selection matters here by an order of magnitude more than it does in public markets, which makes access quality the central question. And how you pace and structure a commitment can change the fee load by hundreds of thousands of dollars without changing a single investment decision.

### The questions worth asking

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|

| How many vintage years does my allocation span? | One year of entry prices can define a decade |
