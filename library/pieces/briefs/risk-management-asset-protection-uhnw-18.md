## One Bad Afternoon Can Reach Assets You Have Not Earned Yet

**For families whose balance sheet has grown faster than the protection around it, which is nearly all of them.**

A jury award above $10 million is called a nuclear verdict. In 2024 those verdicts climbed 52% year over year and totaled $31.3 billion, and the median award among them had already reached $23.8 million the year before.

Set that against the liability limit on a standard homeowners or auto policy, which typically caps at $500,000 to $1 million. The gap is not a rounding error. It is most of a balance sheet. And for a high-net-worth household the exposure does not stop at what you own today, because a judgment can attach to future earnings and to assets you have not yet acquired.

This is the least expensive problem in the entire series to solve, and the one most families have looked at least recently.

## Two Different Jobs

### What changed in liability

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| Definition of a nuclear verdict | Jury award above $10 million |
| Increase over the past decade | 57% |
| Year over year increase, 2024 | 52% |
| Total value of nuclear verdicts, 2024 | $31.3 billion |
| Median nuclear verdict award, 2023 | $23.8 million |
| Typical liability cap on homeowners and auto policies | $500,000 to $1 million |

Plaintiffs' attorneys look for collectible defendants. Wealth does not cause accidents, but it does influence which accidents become lawsuits and how those lawsuits are valued.

### Where the exposure actually comes from

| Source | Why it reaches your balance sheet |
|---|---|
| Auto, including every driver on your policy | The single largest driver of catastrophic personal liability |
| Premises, including pools, dogs, stairs, and driveways | Guests, contractors, and delivery personnel |
| Household employees | Wage, injury, and employment practice claims |
| Serving alcohol at home | Host liability in many states |
| Board and nonprofit service | Personal exposure where the organization's coverage is thin |
| Defamation and social media | Increasingly litigated and frequently uncovered |

### What the 2026 market is doing

| Line | Current condition |
|---|---|
| Umbrella premiums | Increases moderating to 5% to 10% after prior spikes of 15% to 30% |
| Required underlying auto liability | Rising to $500,000 or $1 million from a $250,000 standard |
| Capacity above $25 million | Tight, increasingly placed through surplus lines carriers |
| Households with teenage drivers | Many carriers decline limits above $1 million |
| Homeowners premiums | Projected up 8% to 12% nationally, 15% to 25% in catastrophe-prone states |
| Secondary and vacant homes | Carriers reducing willingness to write at all |

Availability, not price, is the binding constraint this year. Coverage that is easy to buy in a calm year can be unavailable in the year you decide you need it.

### The exposures that did not exist a decade ago

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| Increase in personal deepfake fraud exposure | 3,000% |
| Average US data breach cost | $10.22 million |
| Flood claims originating outside designated high-risk zones | More than 25% |

Cyber policies now address voice cloning, deepfakes, and social engineering aimed at families rather than companies. A convincing call from a familiar voice is the current version of a wire fraud attempt.

### Insurance and structure are different tools

| Approach | What it does | What it cannot do |
|---|---|---|
| Risk transfer through insurance | Pays claims from someone else's balance sheet | Cover excluded claims or exceed the policy limit |
| Segregation through entities and titling | Confines a claim to the asset that produced it | Protect against your own personal negligence |
| Statutory exemption, retirement and homestead | Places certain assets beyond creditor reach by law | Deliver consistent results, since state law varies enormously |
| Trust structures | Removes ownership entirely | Help if established after a claim exists or is foreseeable |

Most families treat these as alternatives. They are layers, and each one covers what the others miss.

### What a rebuilt protection program gives you

- Liability limits sized to your actual balance sheet rather than to what you bought years ago
- Coverage arranged while it is still available, before a claim history or a teenage driver limits your options
- Rental and investment property separated so one incident cannot reach everything
- Cyber and fraud protection covering the impersonation attacks now aimed at families
- A clear understanding of what your state already protects without any action
- Structures put in place during the quiet years, when they are still effective

### Why this matters now

Three trends are compounding. Verdict severity is rising faster than general inflation and shows no sign of reversing. Insurance capacity at high limits is tightening, so the coverage is harder to obtain than it was even two years ago. And property markets in catastrophe-exposed states are contracting, which means secondary homes and coastal properties may become difficult to insure at any price.

None of that is a reason for alarm. It is a reason to review a program that, for most families, was sized to a net worth they passed a long time ago.

## What the Numbers Look Like in Practice

A composite family holds $40 million. There is a teenage driver in the household, a rental property held in their own names, and a $1 million umbrella policy purchased eleven years ago when their net worth was $6 million.

### The cost of no action

A serious auto accident involving a household driver produces an $8 million judgment.

| Measure | Amount |
|---|---|
| Judgment | $8,000,000 |
| Underlying auto policy limit | $500,000 |
| Umbrella limit | $1,000,000 |
| **Total insurance response** | **$1,500,000** |
| **Uninsured judgment** | **$6,500,000** |
| Assets available to satisfy it | Everything not exempt by statute, plus future earnings |

The family did not have a bad insurance policy. They had a policy correctly sized to the life they had eleven years ago.

### The benefit of acting now

| Action | Approximate annual cost |
|---|---|
| Raise underlying auto liability to $1,000,000 | Modest premium increase |
| Build the umbrella tower to $25,000,000 | $12,000 to $20,000 |
| Move the rental property into a separate limited liability company | $2,000 to establish, then a few hundred |
| Add personal cyber and fraud coverage | $1,500 to $3,000 |
| Review homestead and retirement account exemptions with counsel | One-time legal review |

### Side by side

| Measure | Current program | Rebuilt program |
|---|---|---|
| Underlying auto liability | $500,000 | $1,000,000 |
| Umbrella limit | $1,000,000 | $25,000,000 |
| Rental property ownership | Personal | Separate entity |
| Personal cyber coverage | None | In place |
| Total annual cost | About $3,000 | About $25,000 |
| Insurance response to an $8 million judgment | $1,500,000 | $8,000,000 |
| **Uninsured exposure** | **$6,500,000** | **$0** |
| **Additional annual cost** | | **About $22,000** |

Roughly $22,000 a year stands between this family and a $6.5 million exposure. Measured against the estate planning, tax, and investment work in the rest of this series, it is the cheapest risk on the page.

### What the table does not solve

An entity around a rental property confines claims arising at that property. It does not protect that property from a claim arising elsewhere, and it does not shield you from liability for your own negligence. If you were driving, the entity is irrelevant.

Capacity above $25 million is genuinely tight, and a household with a teenage driver may find several carriers unwilling to write past $1 million regardless of assets. That is an argument for reviewing coverage now rather than after the next renewal, and for treating availability as the scarce resource it currently is.

Structures also have to precede the problem. A transfer made after a claim exists or is reasonably foreseeable can be unwound.
