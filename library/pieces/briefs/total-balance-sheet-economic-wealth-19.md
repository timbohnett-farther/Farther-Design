## Understanding Your Total Balance Sheet

### What belongs on it

A total balance sheet lists everything you own and everything you owe, not only what sits at a custodian. Above roughly $10 million of net worth, the custodial statement captures a minority of the real number. The rest is private company equity, directly held real estate, deferred compensation, carried interest, life insurance death benefits, notes receivable, and the debt secured against all of it.

Standard practice adds two entries that never appear on any statement. The first is human capital, the present value of your remaining career earnings. The second is the present value of your committed future spending. A family with $20 million of assets and $25 million of lifetime obligations is in a different position than the headline number suggests.

### The invisible share grows with wealth

| Net worth tier | Private and alternative assets | Private company equity | Debt-to-assets | Avg. primary residence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $2M – $10M | 24% | 6% | 8% | $1.30M |
| $10M – $25M | 31% | not reported | 5% | $2.01M |
| $25M+ | 34% | 21% | 4% | $3.33M |

*Share of total net worth. Debt-to-assets at the lowest tier reflects the all-respondent average.*

### Where that wealth actually sits

| Private and alternative holding | Share of private portfolio |
|---|---|
| Investment real estate | 42% |
| Private company equity | 24% |
| Digital assets | 13% |
| Private credit | 6% |
| Precious metals | 5% |
| Hedge funds | 3% |

### Concentration in your own company

| Household type | Share of private portfolio held in own company |
|---|---|
| Founders and company owners | 61% |
| Employees holding private company equity | 67% |

Concentration built this wealth. That is worth saying plainly. The question is not whether it was right, but how much of it should convert into something durable, and when.

### The cost of the liabilities side

| Debt type | Average rate |
|---|---|
| Primary residence mortgage | 3.4% |
| Investment real estate mortgage | 4.5% |
| Portfolio line of credit | 5.2% |
| Home equity line of credit | 6.1% |

Seventy-three percent of high-net-worth mortgages carry rates below 4%. Families who map this in advance can fund obligations without trading cheap debt for expensive debt.

### What a complete picture gives you

- A real federal exposure number. Every dollar above $30 million for a married couple is taxed at 40%.
- Visibility into assets sitting inside your estate but outside your plan. A personally owned life insurance policy is includable at full death benefit value.
- An answer on IRC §6166. When a closely held business exceeds 35% of the adjusted gross estate, the estate may elect to spread the attributable federal tax over as long as 14 years rather than paying within nine months.
- Your true equity concentration. Public equities average 51% of net worth at this level. Add an operating business and actual exposure runs far higher than the portfolio implies.
- A funding plan for future obligations that protects your existing low-rate debt.
- One document your CPA, your attorney, and your advisor can all work from.

### Why this matters now

The permanent exemption removed a deadline, not the exposure. Asset values keep compounding, state thresholds start as low as $1 million, and roughly half of all projected US wealth transfer through 2048 will come from households in this segment. The families who document early convert a tax problem into a set of choices.

*Figures effective January 1, 2026 under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act; annual gift exclusion remains $19,000 per recipient and the top federal estate tax rate is 40%. Thirteen jurisdictions impose a separate state estate tax with thresholds reported as low as $1 million, though published state figures vary by source and change frequently. Confirm current amounts with your tax and legal advisors.*

## What the Numbers Look Like in Practice

A composite household: married business owners in their mid-fifties, both US citizens, in a state with no separate estate tax. Their quarterly statements show $9 million.

### What the statement shows versus what exists

| Balance sheet item | On the statement | Actual value |
|---|---|---|
| Marketable securities and retirement accounts | $9.0M | $9.0M |
| Operating business interest (60% owner) | not shown | $24.0M |
| Investment real estate, net of debt | not shown | $4.0M |
| Primary residence, net of mortgage | not shown | $2.7M |
| Life insurance death benefit, personally owned | not shown | $4.0M |
| Deferred compensation and vested carry | not shown | $2.3M |
| **Total net worth** | **$9.0M** | **$46.0M** |

The statement captures 20% of the household.

### The cost of no action

Left alone, one 20% business stake compounds inside the taxable estate.

| Year | Value of 20% stake at 6% growth | Cumulative appreciation |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | $4.80M | — |
| 5 | $6.42M | $1.62M |
| 10 | $8.60M | $3.80M |
| 15 | $11.50M | $6.70M |

| If nothing changes | Amount |
|---|---|
| Federal estate tax at second death | $9.1M |
| Attributable to appreciation left inside the estate | $2.7M |
| Attributable to the personally owned death benefit | $1.6M |
| **Total that early action would have avoided** | **$4.3M** |

### The benefit of acting now

| Action taken at 55 | Removed from estate | Federal tax saved |
|---|---|---|
| Death benefit retitled to an irrevocable trust | $4.0M | $1.6M |
| 20% business stake gifted at today's value | $6.7M of appreciation | $2.7M |
| **Combined** | **$10.7M** | **$4.3M** |

### Side by side

| | Wait | Act now |
|---|---|---|
| Assets other than the business stake | $41.2M | $41.2M |
| 20% business stake at second death | $11.5M | excluded, gifted in year 0 |
| Death benefit moved to irrevocable trust | — | ($4.0M) |
| **Gross taxable estate** | **$52.7M** | **$37.2M** |
| Lifetime exemption available | ($30.0M) | ($25.2M) |
| **Taxable estate** | **$22.7M** | **$12.0M** |
| **Federal estate tax at 40%** | **$9.1M** | **$4.8M** |
| **Difference** | | **$4.3M** |

The gift consumes $4.8 million of lifetime exemption, which is why the exemption line falls. The gain comes entirely from moving future appreciation outside the estate and taking the death benefit out of it.

None of this required selling the company, triggering a liquidity event, or changing how the business runs. It required knowing the number early enough to act on it.

*Assumptions: second death fifteen years out, combined exemption held flat at $30 million (inflation indexing would reduce exposure), 40% top rate, 6% annual business growth, all assets other than the gifted stake held at current value, no state estate tax, and no valuation discounts modeled. A qualified appraisal may improve the gifting result materially.*
