Retirement accounts
Taking the first RMD in the current year versus delaying to April 1
An account holder's first RMD can be delayed until April 1 of the following year, but doing so means two RMDs are due in that following year, which can push total taxable income meaningfully higher than spreading the distributions across two separate years.
How it works
Delaying the first RMD seems appealing, but it results in two required distributions landing in the same tax year, potentially pushing income into a higher bracket or triggering other income-based thresholds. Taking the first RMD in the year it's actually due, rather than delaying, often produces a smoother, more manageable tax outcome.
Step 1
Determine the year the first RMD is due, based on the account holder's age.
Step 2
Compare current-year projected income against next year's projected income.
Step 3
Decide whether to take the RMD by December 31 of the current year or delay to April 1 of the following year.
Step 4
If delaying, plan explicitly for two RMDs landing in the same subsequent tax year.

Who this is designed for
Individuals approaching their first required RMD
Households comparing current-year versus next-year income projections
Anyone considering delaying their first RMD without modeling the resulting two-RMD year
Key benefits at a glance
Avoids an unnecessary doubling of taxable income in a single year
Provides a smoother, more predictable tax trajectory
Reduces the risk of triggering higher IRMAA tiers
Preserves flexibility to model both options before deciding
Important considerations
Automatic delay
Automatically delaying the first RMD without modeling the tax impact of two distributions in one year.
Ignoring IRMAA
Failing to consider how the combined RMDs might affect IRMAA or other income-based thresholds.
Missed deadline
Missing the April 1 deadline if delaying was the chosen strategy.
