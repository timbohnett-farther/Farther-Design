Investor behavior · client education guide
Investor behavior
The gap is behavior, not selection
Behavioral biases are predictable, systematic patterns in how people make financial decisions, driven by emotion rather than logic. Morningstar's annual "Mind the Gap" study and DALBAR's Quantitative Analysis of Investor Behavior consistently find that the average investor underperforms the very funds they hold, largely through poorly timed buying and selling.
How it works
Recognizing these patterns in yourself is the first step to building safeguards, such as a written investment plan, that let you act rationally when emotions run high.
Step 1
Write the plan while markets are calm, including what would justify a change.
Step 2
Name your own tendencies honestly, rather than assuming you are immune.
Step 3
Put a delay between the impulse and the trade.
Step 4
Use an objective third party as the check during emotionally charged moments.

Behavioral biases · fit and reference
Five biases to recognize
Loss aversion: losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good, which drives panic-selling
Recency bias: overweighting the latest crash or rally when predicting what comes next
Herd behavior: following what everyone else is doing rather than your own strategy
Overconfidence: believing you can reliably predict market direction
Confirmation bias: seeking evidence for a decision already made, ignoring the rest
What recognizing them buys you
Less likelihood of panic-selling during downturns
Less exposure to hype-driven trends near their peak
More disciplined, rules-based decisions
A written plan that functions as a safeguard rather than a document
Better long-term outcomes simply from less unnecessary trading
Where the damage happens
At the bottom
Selling converts a paper loss into a realized one, at the worst available price.
During the recovery
Waiting for confidence to return means re-entering after much of the rebound.
At the top
Herd behavior and overconfidence push money in exactly when risk is highest.
