Cash flow planning
Compare the guaranteed rate against the expected one
The question is whether extra cash should retire debt faster or be invested for growth. The answer usually comes down to comparing the interest rate you are paying against the return you can reasonably expect.
How it works
High-interest debt is a cost you know with certainty today, one that typical investment returns struggle to beat. Lower-interest debt, such as many mortgages, may be worth paying gradually while investing the difference.
Step 1
List every debt with its rate and whether the interest is deductible.
Step 2
Compare each rate against your expected after-tax investment return.
Step 3
Retire anything above that line first, in rate order.
Step 4
Weigh the psychological value of being debt-free where the math is close.

Debt and investing · fit and reference
What a deliberate approach gives you
High-interest debt eliminated first, where the return is effectively guaranteed
Continued compounding on the lower-rate side of the balance sheet
A rules-based framework instead of a recurring judgment call
Room for the psychological value of being debt-free, not just the arithmetic
Recognition that tax-deductibility changes the effective rate on some debt
Where the math gets closer
Mortgage rates near or below expected after-tax returns
Debt whose interest is deductible, lowering the effective rate
Variable-rate balances that can reprice upward
Any case where paying down debt would leave you without an emergency reserve
Three bands to sort your debt into
Above expected returns
Credit cards and similar. The guaranteed saving beats the expected return, so pay these first.
Near expected returns
Mortgages and some student loans. Either choice is defensible; comfort with debt breaks the tie.
Below expected returns
Low-rate or subsidized debt. Investing the difference generally has the edge over time.
