Portfolio maintenance
Drift raises your risk without asking you
Rebalancing means periodically buying or selling to bring the portfolio back to its target allocation after market movements have shifted it. Strong performers grow into a larger share than intended, quietly raising risk.
How it works
Rebalancing enforces a disciplined sell-high, buy-low behavior, trimming what has grown and adding to what has lagged. That is difficult to do on instinct alone.
Step 1
Define the target allocation and the tolerance band around it.
Step 2
Measure actual weights across all accounts, not one at a time.
Step 3
Trade back to target when a band is breached, or on a set schedule.
Step 4
Execute tax-efficiently, using new contributions and sheltered accounts first.

Rebalancing · fit and reference
What regular rebalancing gives you
Actual risk kept in line with intended risk tolerance
Emotion removed from buy and sell decisions through a rules-based process
Opportunities for tax-loss harvesting when executed thoughtfully
No single holding or asset class allowed to dominate
A natural checkpoint to reassess goals and circumstances
How drift accumulates unnoticed
Long rallies push the winners to a larger share every year
Account-by-account review hides household-level drift
New contributions default into whatever is already largest
The portfolio feels unchanged because nothing was decided
Three ways to trigger a rebalance
On a calendar
Reviewed at a set interval, which is simple and easy to sustain.
On a tolerance band
Triggered when a weight moves more than a set distance from target.
With new money
Directing contributions to the underweight side, which rebalances without selling.
