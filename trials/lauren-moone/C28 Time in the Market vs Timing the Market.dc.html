Investor behavior
The best days cluster right after the worst
Timing the market means predicting short-term moves, buying before a rally and selling before a drop. Time in the market means staying invested through cycles and letting the portfolio compound.
How it works
The market's best days often arrive immediately after its worst, precisely when nervous investors have already sold. Jumping out to dodge volatility usually means missing the recovery.
Step 1
Write the plan before volatility arrives, including what would justify a change.
Step 2
Automate contributions so participation does not depend on conviction.
Step 3
Judge progress against the plan, not against headlines.
Step 4
Use rebalancing as the response to market moves, rather than exiting.

Time in the market · fit and reference
What staying invested captures
The market's long-term upward trend, rather than short-term noise
Avoidance of the double mistake: selling low and buying back high
Less stress and decision fatigue from watching headlines
Uninterrupted compounding, the single biggest driver of long-term wealth
Behavior aligned with a written plan instead of emotion
Why timing rarely works
No one reliably predicts short-term moves, including professional traders
Most attempts to time the market underperform buy-and-hold over the long run
Exiting requires two correct decisions, not one
The days that matter most are unknowable in advance and few in number
Three reasons the exit is costly
Clustering
The best days sit close to the worst, so leaving after a decline usually means missing the rebound.
Two decisions
Timing requires being right about when to leave and when to return.
Compounding
Every interruption removes years of growth on the balance that was withdrawn.
