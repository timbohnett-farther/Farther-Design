Estate planning
A trust is about probate, privacy, and control
Individual ownership titles assets in your name, and they typically pass through probate at death. A revocable living trust holds assets on your behalf during life, allowing them to pass to beneficiaries without probate.
How it works
A revocable trust does not reduce estate taxes or shield assets from your own creditors during your lifetime. Its benefits are probate avoidance, privacy, and control over how and when beneficiaries receive assets.
Step 1
Establish whether probate in your state is costly or straightforward.
Step 2
Identify property in more than one state, which multiplies the process.
Step 3
Create the trust with your attorney where the benefits justify it.
Step 4
Fund it. Retitling the assets is the step most often left undone.

Trusts · fit and reference
What a funded trust provides
Probate avoided, which can take months and enters the public record
Detailed instructions on how and when beneficiaries receive assets
Continuity of management if you become incapacitated, without court involvement
Simpler handling of property held in multiple states
Privacy, since trust terms are generally not public in the way a will is
What a revocable trust does not do
It does not reduce estate taxes
It does not protect assets from your own creditors during your lifetime
It does nothing at all for assets never retitled into it
It may add little where a state offers a simplified small-estate process
Three questions that decide it
How costly is probate here?
Some states offer simplified processes; others are involved and costly regardless of estate size.
Is property in more than one state?
Each state where real property sits can require its own proceeding.
Does timing of inheritance matter?
Staggered distributions for young heirs are something a will alone cannot manage.
