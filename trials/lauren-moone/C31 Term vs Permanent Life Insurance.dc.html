Insurance planning
The mistake is using one job's tool on another job
Term insurance is built primarily for temporary income replacement and liability protection. Permanent insurance is built for a longer-duration problem: estate liquidity, business planning, legacy goals, or coverage expected to remain in force for life.
How it works
The right comparison is not which policy is better. It is which risk you are actually trying to insure. Most families have a temporary income problem first. Some also have a permanent liquidity or transfer problem later.
Step 1
Identify the risk: replacing earned income, or funding a lifetime obligation.
Step 2
Date the need. A mortgage and dependent children both have an end.
Step 3
Solve the temporary problem with term, at the lowest sustainable cost.
Step 4
Reserve permanent coverage for a true long-horizon need, if one exists.

The cost of a mismatch
A 38-year-old couple with two children may need $2 million of protection for the next 20 years, not necessarily for life. Using a permanent policy for a temporary problem may commit an extra $15,000 to $28,000 per year of cash flow that could have gone to retirement savings, debt reduction, or college funding.

Term and permanent · fit and reference
When term is the right answer
The need has an end date: a mortgage, dependent children, a working career
Cash flow is better used for retirement savings, debt reduction, or education funding
The objective is the largest death benefit per dollar of premium
Convertibility preserves the option to add permanent coverage later
When permanent coverage earns its cost
Estate liquidity is needed at an unknown future date
A buy-sell obligation or business continuity plan requires funding
A legacy or charitable objective is expected to outlive any term
Trust-based planning depends on a policy remaining in force for life
Two lanes, two different problems
Income replacement years
Runs from today until the last dependent obligation ends. High coverage, low cost, finite duration.
Lifetime liquidity need
Arrives at an unknown date and never expires. Lower face amount, higher cost, permanent structure.
A layered approach
Term for the first problem, permanent sized only to the second. Most households need the first before the second.

How Farther approaches this
Farther frames the insurance decision in the context of the entire household plan rather than as a standalone product purchase. That means aligning the coverage need with cash-flow priorities, estate objectives, and existing assets before deciding whether term, permanent, or a layered approach makes the most sense.
