Insurance planning
Most households insure the house before the income that pays for it
For most working-age people, the present value of future earnings is one of the largest assets on the balance sheet. Disability coverage exists to protect that earning power if health interrupts the plan.
How it works
The main planning question is not whether disability is possible. It is how long the household could function if earned income stopped for two years, five years, or permanently.
Step 1
Establish what monthly income the household actually runs on.
Step 2
Read the group policy: replacement ratio, caps, and whether benefits are taxable.
Step 3
Measure the shortfall against real obligations, not gross salary.
Step 4
Consider individual coverage for portability and stronger contract definitions.

Income protection · fit and reference
What the policy is really insuring
Earned income, which for most working-age households is the largest asset they own
Debt service that continues whether or not you can work
Savings and retirement contributions that would otherwise stop
Dependent needs already committed to: tuition, care, household support
Where group coverage falls short
It often replaces only part of income, and may cap the monthly benefit
It is generally not portable when you change jobs
Employer-paid benefits are typically taxable, which lowers what you keep
Contract definitions may be weaker than an individual policy's
Three terms that decide the claim
Definition of disability
Own-occupation pays if you cannot do your specific job. Any-occupation is far more restrictive.
Elimination period
The waiting period before benefits begin, commonly 90 days, funded from reserves.
Benefit taxation
Employer-paid premiums generally make benefits taxable; individually paid premiums generally do not.
