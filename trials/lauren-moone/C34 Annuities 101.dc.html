Insurance planning · client education guide
Insurance planning
A guarantee is a contract term, not a feeling
Annuities are often sold with language that sounds simpler than the contract really is. At their core they are insurance contracts designed to address one or more of three concerns: protected principal, tax deferral, or predictable income.
How it works
An annuity is not automatically good or bad. It may be useful when the planning problem is specific enough: guaranteed income, longevity risk, or a disciplined floor under part of the retirement plan.
Step 1
Name the problem the contract is meant to solve, in one sentence.
Step 2
Read the crediting mechanics: caps, spreads, participation rates, rider fees.
Step 3
Price the liquidity you are giving up, including the surrender schedule.
Step 4
Compare it against the simpler alternative that solves the same problem.

Where an annuity can earn its place
A guaranteed income floor under essential retirement spending
A hedge against outliving other retirement resources
Principal stability for a portion of the plan that cannot tolerate loss
Tax deferral where other tax-advantaged capacity is exhausted
Terms clients most often misunderstand
Cap: the maximum credited rate, regardless of how the index performs
Spread and participation rate: further reductions applied before crediting
Rider: an optional benefit carrying its own annual fee
Annuitization: the point at which the lump sum converts to income and liquidity largely ends
Surrender charge: the cost of exiting during the contract's early years
Contract anatomy, in four parts
Premium
What goes in, either as a lump sum or over time.
Surrender period
The years during which exiting costs money. Usually the least understood term at purchase.
Rider costs
Annual fees for optional guarantees, which reduce what the contract credits.
Payout phase
When and how income begins, and what liquidity remains once it does.
