Insurance planning · client education guide
Insurance planning
A $480,000 care event can become a $600,000 wealth impact
Long-term care planning is not only about nursing homes. It is about the possibility that care may be needed for months or years, whether at home, in assisted living, or in a skilled setting.
How it works
The financial issue is not merely the average cost of care. It is whether the balance sheet can absorb a multi-year care event without forcing asset sales, burdening family members, or derailing a surviving spouse's plan.
Step 1
Estimate a realistic care cost for your area and the type of care you would want.
Step 2
Stress-test a four-year event against portfolio liquidity, not net worth.
Step 3
Decide honestly whether to retain the risk or transfer it.
Step 4
If transferring, compare traditional coverage against a hybrid on the economics.

Long-term care · fit and reference
Three ways families address the risk
Self-fund: reserve capital and accept the possibility of a large future expense
Traditional long-term care insurance: a dedicated pool of benefits for covered care
Hybrid solutions: life insurance or annuity features combined with care benefits
Any of the three can be right; the wrong answer is assuming the risk away
What each approach requires
Self-funding works only if liquidity, return assumptions, and family capacity have been stress-tested
Traditional coverage introduces premium, underwriting, and benefit-design considerations
Premium increases may be permitted on some forms of coverage, subject to regulatory approval
Hybrids appeal to those who dislike paying for a benefit they may never use, but the economics still need review
Three policy terms that decide the payout
Benefit period
How long benefits last once they begin, which caps the total pool available.
Elimination period
The waiting period before benefits start, funded out of pocket.
Inflation rider
Whether the benefit grows with care costs, which have historically outpaced general inflation.
