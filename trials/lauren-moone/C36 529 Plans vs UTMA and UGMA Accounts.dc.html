Education funding
At 18 to 21, a UTMA account becomes the child's money, with no strings attached
A 529 plan is a tax-advantaged account restricted to education expenses. A UTMA or UGMA is a custodial account that can hold almost any asset for a minor's benefit, for any purpose, once the child reaches the age of majority.
How it works
The two vehicles solve different problems. A 529 maximizes tax efficiency for education specifically and keeps the parent in control indefinitely. A UTMA offers investment flexibility but hands the child full, unrestricted control at a legally defined age, typically 18 to 21 depending on the state.
Step 1
Decide whether the money is for education specifically or for a broader head start.
Step 2
Check your state's 529 deduction and your state's age of majority for custodial accounts.
Step 3
Weigh the financial aid treatment, which differs by a factor of roughly four.
Step 4
Fund both where the goals genuinely differ, rather than forcing one to do two jobs.
The key insight
The decision usually comes down to one question: how much control do you want to keep, and how confident are you that the money will actually be used the way you intend once your child is legally an adult?
Where the 529 wins
Tax efficiency and control for costs clearly identifiable as education, especially where the state offers a meaningful deduction
Financial aid treatment, in nearly every case, since parent-owned assets are assessed far less heavily
Where the UTMA wins
Flexibility when the goal is broader than education: a first home down payment, a business start, a general head start
Investment choice, since 529 plans restrict investors to a limited menu chosen by the plan sponsor

A note on the kiddie tax
Investment earnings inside a UTMA or UGMA are subject to the kiddie tax: a portion of unearned income is taxed at the child's typically lower rate, but income above an annual threshold is taxed at the parent's marginal rate. This can meaningfully reduce the tax efficiency of a custodial account for a family that expected the child's lower bracket to apply to all the growth.
