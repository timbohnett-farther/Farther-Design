The plan is finished. The family is not
Almost every family at this level has documents. Trusts are drafted, entities formed, exemptions allocated, and the tax modeled. That work is also responsible for a small minority of what goes wrong.

Small families do not need all of this. Most need a charter, a regular meeting, and a written answer to who decides what.
The questions that need an owner before they need an answer
Who may work in the family business, and on what terms
How and when distributions are made, and on what basis
Whether a family member may sell an interest, and to whom
How disagreements are resolved without going to court
Who selects, monitors, and replaces the trustee
What information each generation receives, and at what age
If the answer to any of these is blank, or reads "Dad," the family has a plan for its assets and no plan for itself.

What this table cannot claim
Governance does not guarantee a good outcome. Families with excellent structures still have painful disagreements, and some transition beautifully with nothing written down. What a structure changes is the odds, and what it provides is a process that exists before the disagreement does. Conflict arrives in every family eventually, and it is far cheaper to decide how you will handle it while nobody is angry.

Three points carry the piece
The technical work that consumes almost all of the planning conversation accounts for a small share of what actually goes wrong. The most reliable predictor of a successful transition is whether the family talks to each other, and most families at this level do not. And governance is not a document; it is a set of answers to who decides what, written down while everyone is still on speaking terms.
How Farther approaches this
Most advisors stop at the edge of the family conversation, because the technical work is what they were hired for and the human work is uncomfortable. There is no product at the end of this conversation. The Intelligent Wealth Platform gives the family one shared view of what exists, which is often the first time everyone has seen the same picture.
What that looks like in practice
Facilitating the first conversation, which is usually the hardest and rarely happens on its own
Helping the family write down who decides what, before circumstances force an answer
Building age-appropriate financial education over years, not in a single meeting
Bringing in independent voices where a family member should not be the only judge
Coordinating governance with the trust, entity, and succession structures so they do not contradict
