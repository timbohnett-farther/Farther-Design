Judgment is learned. It does not arrive with the money

The other departures are not about returns. They are about a relationship that never included the person who eventually inherited.

What this illustration assumes
It assumes preparation changes behavior, which is a reasonable expectation and not a certainty. Prepared heirs sometimes make poor decisions and unprepared heirs sometimes do well. The withdrawal rates and returns shown represent plausible outcomes at each end rather than measured averages. What is not an assumption is that a person who has never made a consequential financial decision will be making one for the first time with four million dollars.

Three points carry the piece
Financial literacy and financial capability are different things, and only the second survives a large inheritance. Heirs who leave their parents' advisor almost never leave over performance, which means the relationship, not the returns, is what failed to transfer. And preparation is a decade of small decisions with real consequences, which means it starts long before anyone thinks it is necessary.
How Farther approaches this
Most firms meet the next generation at the reading of a will. There is no minimum that makes a twenty-five year old with a $250,000 account uninteresting, and no product that makes them interesting for the wrong reason. Advisors freed from administrative work have time to sit with your children more than once.
What that looks like in practice
Meeting your children years before any distribution, at an age when the conversation is low stakes
Managing small, real accounts so decisions have consequences at a survivable scale
Building a disclosure schedule with you, so what gets shared and when is a decision rather than a default
Introducing heirs to the trustee, attorney, and accountant while everyone can still explain
Reviewing distribution provisions against how your children are actually turning out
